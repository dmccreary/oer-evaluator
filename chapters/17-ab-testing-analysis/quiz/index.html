    3. 17. Analyzing A/B Test Results

    Quiz: Analyzing A/B Test Results¶

    Test your understanding of data collection, anonymized data, means, effect size, surveys, results summaries, pilot tests, and equity with these review questions.

    1. What does Cohen's d measure?¶

    1. The probability that the two groups differ by chance alone, given the sample
    2. The share of students who finished the activity out of those who started it
    3. The average of the two groups' post-test scores, taken together as one number
    4. How large a difference is, as the difference of means over the pooled standard deviation
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. An effect size measures the size of a difference in units that do not depend on the test, so that studies can be compared. Cohen's d divides the difference of means by the pooled standard deviation, which combines the spread of the two groups. In the Watershed Basics pilot, a difference of 7.0 points and a pooled standard deviation of about 6.78 give a d of about 1.03. Chance is a different question, and d does not answer it.

    Concept Tested: Effect Size

    See: Effect Size

    2. How is a completion rate calculated?¶

    1. The number of students who started divided by the number who were invited, as a percentage
    2. The minutes of work divided by the intended length of the quiz, as a percentage
    3. The number of students who finished divided by the number who started, as a percentage
    4. The post-test mean divided by the pre-test mean, as a percentage of the class
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. In the pilot, 10 of the 12 control students finished the quiz, a rate of 83 percent, and all 12 treatment students did, a rate of 100 percent. A low rate is a signal to look for the reason. The two control students who did not finish could not make the original quiz load on the school's tablets, which is a barrier and not a lack of interest. The post-test was separate from the quiz, so those students stayed in the analysis.

    See: Completion Rate

    3. How does anonymized data differ from coded data?¶

    1. Coded data keeps a key that can rebuild the link to a person, while anonymized data has no such link
    2. Anonymized data replaces names with codes, while coded data removes them completely from every table in the study
    3. Coded data can never be shared with anyone outside the research team or school
    4. Anonymized data still contains names but hides the scores of each individual student
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Coded data replaces names with codes and keeps a key somewhere, so the link can be rebuilt. Data becomes anonymized when the key is destroyed on the deletion date promised in the consent letter and nothing else in the table points to a person. Codes alone are not always enough: in a class of 24, columns for gender, period, and teacher can single out a student. Ask whether someone who knows the class could pick out one student.

    Concept Tested: Anonymized Data

    See: Anonymized Data

    4. Why does the chapter compare the groups' mean gains and not only their post-test means?¶

    1. Post-test means cannot be expressed in percentage points, so they cannot be compared
    2. Gains are always easier to compute than post-test means, so they save time
    3. Post-test means can never be negative, so they hide a loss in learning
    4. The groups may not start at the same level, and the gain removes any head start
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. In the pilot, the post-test means differ by 7.75 points, but the groups began 0.75 points apart, so the gain removes that head start. The difference of means, treatment minus control, is the single number that answers the hypothesis. Here it is 22.0 minus 15.0, or +7.0 points, above the 5-point bar. In a small test a few unusually strong or weak students can move an average, so the difference may partly be luck.

    Concept Tested: Difference of Means

    See: Difference of Means

    5. A test finds a difference of means of 5.0 points and a pooled standard deviation of 10.0 points. What are Cohen's d and its conventional size?¶

    1. 0.20, small
    2. 0.50, medium
    3. 2.00, large
    4. 0.80, large
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Cohen's d is the difference of means divided by the pooled standard deviation, so 5.0 divided by 10.0 is 0.50. By convention, 0.2 is small, 0.5 is medium, and 0.8 is large. These are rules of thumb, and what matters in practice depends on the cost of the change and the setting. The same 5-point difference would be a larger effect if the scores varied less, which is why the spread is part of the answer.

    Concept Tested: Effect Size

    See: Effect Size

    6. Five control students gained 10, 12, 15, 18, and 20 points. Five treatment students gained 16, 18, 22, 26, and 28 points. What is the difference of means, treatment minus control?¶

    1. +5.0
    2. +6.0
    3. +7.0
    4. +22.0
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The control gains add to 75, so their mean is 75 divided by 5, which is 15.0. The treatment gains add to 110, so their mean is 110 divided by 5, which is 22.0. The difference of means is 22.0 minus 15.0, which is +7.0 points. Option D is the treatment mean alone. A mean hides how scores are spread out, so also report the standard deviation and the median.

    Concept Tested: Mean Score

    See: Mean Score

    7. A pilot finds an effect size of 1.03. A teacher uses the rule of 16 divided by d squared and plans a larger test with 16 students per group. What is wrong with this plan?¶

    1. Pilot effect sizes tend to be inflated, so plan on about half, which calls for about 64 students per group
    2. The rule works only when d is larger than 1.5, so it cannot be used for this pilot or for any other
    3. The pilot's effect size is too small to use for planning, so a larger one is needed from some other study
    4. The rule produces too many students when d is large, so the number must be cut to fit the budget
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Impressive estimates from small tests tend to shrink when repeated with more students, so a pilot's own effect size is too optimistic to plan on. A safer habit is to plan for about half of it, close to 0.5, and 16 divided by 0.25 gives 64 students per group. For comparison, detecting a small effect of 0.2 needs about 400 per group. The rule is an approximation, and a statistician should confirm the numbers for a real study.

    Concept Tested: Pilot Test

    See: Pilot Test

    8. Three resources each have a rubric total of 65, and their mean normalized gains are 0.22, 0.28, and 0.33. The resource with the lowest gain has an Accuracy score of 3. What does this show?¶

    1. The rubric has no relationship with learning, because equal totals gave different gains
    2. Equal totals do not guarantee equal learning, and the pattern supports reading the lowest metric first
    3. Rubric totals cause learning gains, because higher totals went with higher gains
    4. The correlation between totals and gains must be exactly 1 for the rubric to be valid
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Across the seven resources in the chapter's table, the correlation between totals and gains is about 0.93, so higher totals go with higher gains. A correlation does not show cause (C), and it can swing widely with only seven points, so a serious check needs 20 or more resources. The three equal totals with different gains show that a total hides the weakest metric, which is the lesson the chapters on the rubric kept repeating.

    Concept Tested: Rubric-Outcome Correlation

    See: Rubric-Outcome Correlation

    9. A draft results summary for the pilot says, "The simulation caused the larger gain." The pilot tested five fixes together as a bundle. What is the best judgment of this claim?¶

    1. It is supported, because the simulation was the biggest change made to the resource
    2. It is supported, because the effect size was large enough to show which fix mattered
    3. It is supported, because the treatment group's gain was larger than the control group's gain in the pilot
    4. It is not shown by this test, because the fixes arrived together and one cannot be singled out
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. A test of two versions with five fixes tests the bundle. It can say whether the bundle helped students, and it cannot say which fix did the work. To learn that, test one fix at a time, which takes more students and more time. A results summary claims no more than the data allow, so its Limitations part names the small sample, the bundle of fixes, and anything unusual. A large pilot effect size is a reason to test again.

    Concept Tested: Results Summary

    See: Results Summary

    10. A teacher wants to build an equity check into the next test of the improved resource. Which plan best follows the chapter?¶

    1. Report separate averages for the 2 students who used captions and the 3 students learning English
    2. Confirm before the test that all students can use both versions, ask about barriers, and use comments for tiny groups
    3. Assume both versions work for everyone, because students were assigned by chance to each version
    4. Leave out students who use assistive technology so that the two groups stay more similar
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. An equity consideration is a check that the resource, and the test of it, work fairly for all students, including those who use assistive technology, are learning English, or lack a home device or a fast connection. Build the check in before the test, ask about barriers in the survey, and record observations. Groups smaller than about ten students should not be reported as averages, because they can identify students. Excluding students (D) is an equity failure.
