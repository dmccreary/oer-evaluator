    Narrative Prompt This is a fictional comedy for adult readers: OER evangelists, librarians, instructional designers, and the professors and teachers who choose textbooks. It is set at a Minnesota-style county fair and, on the following Monday, in a college faculty office. The central idea comes from Chapter 3 of the book: a resource has two independent halves, quality and reusability, and they must be scored and reported separately. A great dish that nobody can copy is a "good but locked" resource. A flawless recipe card for a bland dish is an "open but weak" resource. The dish that wins both columns is "strong and open". Characters: Margie Halvorsen, the volunteer judge, is a librarian and OER evangelist. Chet Olson is the fair board chairman, who wants one number. The three contest cooks are Ardis (Entry A, the delicious dish with a secret napkin recipe), Wendell (Entry B, the perfectly documented dish that tastes like cardboard), and Dolores with the church basement committee (Entry C, the winner). On Monday, Dr. Priya Raman, a chemistry professor, compares two chemistry textbooks with Margie. Echo the Loon is a tiny background cameo in several panels. Illustrative numbers: all scores in this story are illustrative. The fair's score sheet is a playful stand-in for the book's rubric, with a Taste column and a Recipe column of 10 points each; the Recipe column stands in for the five reusability metrics (open license, modularity, metadata, editable source, portability). The casserole scores are 9 and 2 (Entry A), 2 and 9 (Entry B), and 9 and 9 (Entry C). The two textbooks have quality and reusability subtotals of 44 and 12 (Text A) and 20 and 40 (Text B). These mirror the style of the eight profiles in Chapter 3's Quality and Reusability Quadrant Sorter, and each subtotal is out of 50. The totals, 56 and 60, both fall in the Fair band (50 to 74) from Chapter 3. The quadrant boundary of 25 per subtotal is a teaching choice, not a standard. Visual language: county-fair comic style in the book's flat vector house style, with indigo and burnt orange accents. Keep every character's look identical across panels. The same recurring gags appear throughout: a padlock hanging on anything locked, an open padlock on anything shareable, and two-bar scoreboards that are never merged into one.

    Prologue – Two Questions Every Judge Gets¶

    Every county fair judge hears the same two questions the moment the ribbon goes on: "Is it any good?" and "Can I have the recipe?" Most people think those are one question. Margie Halvorsen, librarian, OER evangelist, and volunteer judge of this year's hotdish contest, knows they are two, and she has brought a lanyard to prove it.

    Epilogue – What Did Margie Learn?¶

    A county fair and a faculty office turn out to run on the same rule. Quality asks whether a resource does its job for the person using it. Reusability asks whether other people can find it, change it, and use it in their own setting. A resource can win one scoreboard and lose the other, and a single blended number will hide which one needs work.

    Challenge How Margie Responded Lesson for Today
    The fair wanted one number She crossed out Total and drew two columns Keep the quality subtotal and the reusability subtotal separate. A merged number tells you how good a resource is on average and hides what to fix.
    Entry A: delicious, but locked Scored Taste 9 and Recipe 2, then wrote the recipe down Good but locked: improve the license, metadata, source, or portability. These often change quickly with small actions.
    Entry B: documented, but bland Scored Taste 2 and Recipe 9 Open but weak: improve accuracy, readability, accessibility, or practice. Quality fixes usually take longer.
    A tie at 11 and 11 Held up both sheets instead of flipping a coin The same total can mean opposite problems. Report the total with both subtotals and the lowest metric.
    Two chemistry textbooks on Monday Plotted both on a quadrant Totals of 56 and 60 look alike, but the quadrants called for different plans.

    Call to Action¶

    The next time someone tells you a textbook is "pretty good," ask which scoreboard they mean. Score the resource on quality, score it on reusability, and keep the two numbers side by side. Then bring a hotdish to the meeting and a recipe card to go with it.
