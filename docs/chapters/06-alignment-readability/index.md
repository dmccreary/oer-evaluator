## Learning Outcomes and Objectives

A **learning outcome** is a statement of what a learner will be able to do after instruction. The key words are "be able to do." An outcome describes a capability that someone could demonstrate, not a topic that the course will mention. "Cells" is a topic. "Compare plant and animal cells" is an outcome. Outcomes matter because they decide what a good resource is: a resource is good for a course when it helps learners reach that course's outcomes.

Sam's two-week unit has three outcomes, which first appeared on the review context card in Chapter 3. Learners will identify the main organelles and their functions, compare plant and animal cells, and explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves a cell. The table shows how a topic becomes an outcome, which is the move that makes alignment checkable.

| Topic | Outcome |
|-------|---------|
| Organelles | Identify the main organelles and their functions |
| Plant and animal cells | Compare plant and animal cells |
| The cell membrane | Explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves a cell |

A unit usually has three to six outcomes. With fewer, the unit is probably too vague to review against, and more than six usually means that some are really objectives. Outcomes come from your syllabus, your curriculum guide, or your standards. If you do not have them in writing, write them before you review, because a review without outcomes can only judge quality in general, not fit.

A **learning objective** is a more specific, measurable statement of what a learner will do in a single lesson or activity. Outcomes are broad and describe the end of a unit or course. Objectives are narrow and describe a step along the way. A well-built objective names who is learning, what they will do, under what condition, and to what standard. Take Sam's second outcome. It can be broken into two objectives that fit single lessons, as the table shows.

| Outcome | Objective for one lesson |
|---------|--------------------------|
| Compare plant and animal cells | Students will list three structures found in plant cells but not in animal cells, using a labeled diagram |
| Compare plant and animal cells | Students will explain in two sentences why plant cells have a cell wall and animal cells do not |

A useful check is that an objective has four parts, often remembered as ABCD. The *audience* says who is learning. The *behavior* is the measurable verb and what it acts on. The *condition* says what help or limits apply, and the *degree* says how well it must be done. The table takes apart the first objective above.

| Part | Example |
|------|---------|
| Audience | Students |
| Behavior | List three structures found in plant cells but not in animal cells |
| Condition | Using a labeled diagram |
| Degree | Three structures, all correct |

Objectives that lack a degree are common, and adding one is what makes an objective testable.

A **measurable verb** is an action verb that describes something an observer can see or check, such as list, label, explain, or calculate. Verbs like "understand," "know," and "appreciate" are not measurable, because you cannot watch someone understand. The test is simple: can you picture the learner doing it, and could another teacher tell whether they had? If not, replace the verb. "Understand the cell membrane" becomes "explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell."

The table gives more swaps for vague verbs.

| Vague | Measurable replacement |
|-------|------------------------|
| Understand osmosis | Explain osmosis using an example |
| Know the organelles | Name six organelles and state what each does |
| Appreciate cell diversity | Describe two ways cells differ in form and function |
| Be familiar with the microscope | Focus a microscope and sketch an onion cell |

!!! mascot-tip "Picture It"
    ![Echo the Loon giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    For every outcome you write, picture a student doing it. If you can't see what they would be doing, the verb is too vague, so swap it for one you could watch, such as list, explain, calculate, or compare.

## Bloom's Taxonomy and the Cognitive Levels

Outcomes differ in how much thinking they demand. **Bloom's Taxonomy** is a framework that sorts learning goals by the type of thinking required. It was developed by a committee led by Benjamin Bloom in the 1950s and revised in 2001 by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl, and the 2001 revision is the one this book uses. Its value for a reviewer is practical: it lets you check whether a resource asks learners to think at the level the outcome requires.

The **Bloom cognitive levels** are the six levels of the revised taxonomy, running from simple recall to original work: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create. Each level has typical verbs, which a reviewer can use as a quick test of what an outcome or a question demands. The table lists them with an example from Sam's unit.

| Level | What the learner does | Typical verbs | Example from the cell unit |
|-------|----------------------|---------------|----------------------------|
| Remember | Recalls facts and terms | list, define, name, identify | List the organelles found in a plant cell |
| Understand | Explains ideas in their own words | explain, summarize, classify | Explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves |
| Apply | Uses a procedure in a new situation | use, calculate, demonstrate | Calculate the surface-area-to-volume ratio of two cells |
| Analyze | Breaks something into parts and relates them | differentiate, examine, distinguish | Differentiate the functions of the nucleus and the mitochondria |
| Evaluate | Judges something against criteria | judge, critique, justify | Judge which of two models of the membrane better explains a result |
| Create | Produces something new | design, construct, develop | Design an experiment to test how salt affects osmosis |

## The Alignment Metric

The **alignment metric** is metric 2 of the ten. It asks whether a resource's content, activities, and assessments match the learning outcomes, and at the right level of thinking. It is scored on the review context card: the outcomes you wrote down before reading. The descriptors below are illustrative.

| Score range | Alignment descriptor |
|-------------|----------------------|
| 9 to 10 | Every outcome is covered at the level it requires; assessments match; little irrelevant content |
| 7 to 8 | Every outcome is covered, with minor mismatches in level or emphasis |
| 5 to 6 | An outcome is missing or thinly covered, or the assessments test a lower level than the outcomes require |
| 3 to 4 | Several outcomes are missing or the resource mostly misses the course's purpose |
| 0 to 2 | The resource does not match the outcomes at all |

Scoring starts from a matrix that lines up each outcome against where the resource covers it. The table is Sam's matrix for the open chapter.

| Outcome | Where the chapter covers it | Level the outcome requires | Level the chapter teaches | Finding |
|---------|-----------------------------|----------------------------|----------------------------|---------|
| Identify the main organelles and their functions | Sections 2 and 3 | Remember | Understand | Covered |
| Compare plant and animal cells | Nowhere | Understand | None | Not covered |
| Explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves | Section 4 | Understand | Understand | Covered |

The matrix gives Sam the evidence for a score of 5. One outcome out of three is missing entirely, and the chapter's three end-of-chapter questions only ask learners to list, define, and name, which is a lower level than the third outcome requires. A resource that matches only some of the outcomes can still be useful, but only if the missing parts can be supplied.

Five problems come up again and again when a reviewer fills in the matrix:

- **A missing outcome.** Nothing in the resource teaches it, as with Sam's comparison of plant and animal cells.
- **Content nobody needs.** A section on a topic outside the unit takes learners' time without serving an outcome.
- **The wrong level.** The resource teaches at the recall level when the outcome needs explaining, or the reverse.
- **A mismatched assessment.** The questions test something other than the outcomes.
- **A mismatched order.** The sections come in an order that fights the course's sequence.

Four ideas help a reviewer fill in the matrix. **Outcome coverage** is the share of a course's outcomes that a resource addresses at the required level. Sam's chapter covers 2 of 3, about 67 percent. A **content gap** is an outcome, or part of one, that the resource does not cover. The comparison of plant and animal cells is Sam's gap. A gap is not a reason to reject a resource, because an open license lets Sam fill it: add a section, remix a diagram from another open source, or write an activity. **Assessment alignment** is the match between what the resource asks learners to do and what the outcomes require. Sam's end questions, which only ask for recall, are misaligned with an outcome that requires explaining. And a reviewer should check what a resource *teaches*, not only what its title or table of contents says.

**Standards alignment** is the match between a resource and an external set of standards, such as the academic standards your state or district requires. Standards are written at a general level, so the work is to find which of the resource's content addresses each standard that applies. Suppose a state standard reads, "Students describe the structure and function of the main cell organelles and how cells exchange materials with their surroundings." This is an illustrative standard, not a real one. Sam's chapter addresses both parts, through Sections 2 to 4. Standards alignment matters when your school requires it. Where it does not, the learning outcomes come first, and checking a publisher's claim that a resource "meets the standards" against the actual content is a task for the reviewer, not a courtesy to the publisher.

The table shows the check for the illustrative standard above, one part at a time.

| Part of the standard | Where the chapter addresses it | Finding |
|----------------------|--------------------------------|---------|
| Structure of the main cell organelles | Sections 2 and 3 | Addressed |
| Function of the main cell organelles | Sections 2 and 3 | Addressed |
| How cells exchange materials with their surroundings | Section 4 | Addressed |

    ![Echo the Loon warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A resource that says it is "aligned to the standards" may only list standard numbers on its cover. Open the content and find the section that teaches each standard, and if you can't find one, count it as a gap.

A **curriculum map** is a table that shows where each outcome or standard is taught across a course, and with which resources. It gives the reviewer the context for a resource: what comes before it, what comes after, and which outcomes it is expected to carry. Sam's map for the two-week unit shows where the open chapter fits and where the gap sits.

| Week | Outcome | Resource |
|------|---------|----------|
| 1 | Identify the main organelles and their functions | Open chapter, Sections 2 and 3, plus a microscope lab |
| 2 | Compare plant and animal cells | Gap: needs an activity or a remixed diagram |
| 2 | Explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves | Open chapter, Section 4 |

A quick map takes about fifteen minutes. List the outcomes down the left side, list the resources across the top, and mark which resource covers which outcome. Any outcome with no mark is a gap, and any resource with no mark is a candidate for removal.

A map also shows order. The **scope and sequence** of a course is the list of topics it covers, which is the scope, and the order in which it teaches them, which is the sequence. A resource has to fit the order as well as the content. Sam's unit teaches organelles first and the membrane last, but the open chapter introduces the membrane in Section 2 and organelles after it. Sam could assign Sections 3 and 4 before Section 2, but only because the chapter's sections stand fairly well alone, a property the Modularity metric measures in Chapter 9.

When the sequences do not match, there are four options:

- Assign the sections in your order, if the resource is modular enough to allow it.
- Add a short bridge that carries readers across the jump.
- Split the resource and use only the parts that fit.
- Choose a resource that follows your order.

**Prerequisite knowledge** is what a learner must already know to benefit from a resource. If a resource assumes knowledge the learners lack, they struggle however good the content is. A reviewer lists what the resource assumes and checks it against what the learners know. The table shows the check for Sam's ninth graders.

| What the chapter assumes | Do Sam's students have it? | Action |
|--------------------------|----------------------------|--------|
| What diffusion is | Yes, from an earlier unit | None |
| What lipids and proteins are | No, they come in the next unit | Add a short bridging explanation or glossary entries |
| How to read a labeled diagram | Yes | None |

To find out what learners already know, a short pre-check is better than a guess. Three or four questions at the start of the unit, such as asking students to define diffusion or name a protein, show which assumptions hold. The resource's own preface sometimes lists what it assumes. When it does not, reading the first section with your learners in mind will usually reveal it.

## The Readability Metric

The **readability metric** is metric 3 of the ten. It asks whether the intended audience can read and understand a resource. It is a fit metric, like Alignment. The same text can be readable for a college class and too hard for ninth graders. The descriptors from Chapter 3 apply, and Sam's scores come from three kinds of evidence: a measured reading level, the vocabulary, and the structure of the text.

Sam's evidence for a score of 5 is in the table. A score in the 5 to 6 range means the reading level is one or two grades off and some terms are used before they are defined.

| Evidence | Finding |
|----------|---------|
| Reading level of three sampled passages | About 10.5, which is 1.5 grades above ninth grade |
| Technical terms | Several used before they are defined, including "phospholipid bilayer" |
| Text structure | One 900-word section has no subheadings |

A measured level is only part of the picture, so add a human check. Ask two or three students to read one paragraph aloud and then say in their own words what it meant. Where they stumble or go blank is where the text loses them, and that is better evidence than any formula. Sam tried this with two ninth graders on the paragraph that uses "phospholipid bilayer," and both stopped at the term.

**Reading level** is the grade, or the difficulty, at which a text is written. It is estimated from two features that can be measured: how long the sentences are, and how long or hard the words are. Reading level is a screening tool. It tells you that a text is likely to be hard, not that a particular reader will find it hard. Other measures exist, including the commercial Lexile measure, but the common free ones use the same two features.

For this book's rubric, a text is a good fit when it sits within about one grade of the audience. Mixed groups call for aiming at the lower end of the range, and technical subjects may justify a slightly higher level when the terms are defined. Grade-level formulas are built for English text, and they will mislead on text in another language.

A **readability formula** is a calculation that estimates a text's difficulty from measurable features. The best known is the **Flesch-Kincaid grade level**, which estimates the U.S. school grade needed to read a text. It uses three counts from a passage: the number of sentences, the number of words, and the number of syllables. Before the formula, here is what the parts mean. Words per sentence is the number of words divided by the number of sentences. Syllables per word is the number of syllables divided by the number of words. The formula combines them:

```text
grade level = 0.39 × (words ÷ sentences) + 11.8 × (syllables ÷ words) − 15.59
```

Take the first of Sam's three sampled passages, which has 5 sentences, 100 words, and 153 syllables. Words per sentence is 100 ÷ 5 = 20, and syllables per word is 153 ÷ 100 = 1.53. Then 0.39 × 20 = 7.8, and 11.8 × 1.53 = 18.054, and 7.8 + 18.054 − 15.59 = 10.264, which rounds to a grade level of 10.3. Sam's other two passages came out at 10.6 and 10.7, an average of about 10.5, which is one and a half grades above ninth grade.

Formulas have limits that a reviewer should keep in mind. They measure only length, so they cannot tell whether a short sentence is clear or whether an idea is hard. They work best on samples of 100 words or more of ordinary paragraphs, and they are unreliable on tables, lists, and formulas. And they need a target to compare against: the useful result is the gap between the text's level and your students' grade. Word processors and online tools calculate the score automatically, and Chapter 13 shows how an automated readability scan can do it for a whole resource.

## Vocabulary and Defined Terms

**Vocabulary level** is how common or demanding the words in a text are. A biology chapter must use technical words, and that is not a flaw. The question for a reviewer is how those words are introduced. A technical term is fine when the text defines it and uses it consistently. A term is a problem when it arrives with no definition, or when the definition appears long after the first use.

Sam counted the technical terms in Section 2 of the open chapter. In a two-page stretch there were 14, and 6 were never defined, including "phospholipid bilayer," which first appears in the opening paragraph and is explained only in the fourth. Those figures are illustrative. A ninth grader who meets the term in paragraph one has four paragraphs in which to feel lost. The table lists three of the terms and the fix for each.

| Term | Where it first appears | Where it is defined | Fix |
|------|------------------------|---------------------|-----|
| Phospholipid bilayer | Paragraph 1 | Paragraph 4 | Define it at first use |
| Selectively permeable | Section 4 | Never | Add a definition at first use |
| Hydrophobic | Section 4 | Never | Add a definition at first use, or a glossary entry |

A simple measure is the share of technical terms that are never defined. Sam's count of 6 undefined terms out of 14 is about 43 percent. A resource that defines nearly every technical term at first use is doing its job, and one that leaves close to half undefined will lose many readers.

A **defined term** is a technical word that a text explains where it first uses it, usually by giving the meaning in plain words right beside it. "The cell membrane is a phospholipid bilayer, a double layer of fat-based molecules" defines the term in the sentence that introduces it. A **glossary** is an alphabetical list of the terms in a resource with a short definition of each. It serves learners who forget a term and reviewers who want to see which terms the author thought needed defining. Sam's chapter has no glossary, which an improvement plan can add.

Two related ideas affect vocabulary. **Plain language** is writing that a reader can understand the first time: short sentences, common words, and the active voice. It does not mean simple ideas. A complex idea can be written plainly. **Jargon** is specialized language that a reader does not need or has not been taught. A technical term the learner must learn is not jargon. The word "utilize" where "use" would do is. The table shows an example of each fix.

| Before | After | What changed |
|--------|-------|--------------|
| The aforementioned organelle utilizes oxygen in order to facilitate the generation of ATP. | The mitochondrion uses oxygen to make ATP. | Jargon removed; sentence shortened |
| It is the case that the membrane is permeable to certain substances. | The membrane lets some substances through. | Plain language; shorter |

## Text Structure and Audience Fit

**Text structure** is how a text is organized: its sections, paragraphs, lists, and the signals that tell readers where they are. A well-structured text lets a reader skim and find things. A poorly structured one forces them to read linearly and hold everything in mind. Sam's Section 3 is one 900-word block with no subheadings. Splitting it into four short subsections, one for each organelle group, costs nothing and makes the section easier to read, to skim, and later to reuse. The before-and-after of that change is in the table.

| | Before | After |
|-|--------|-------|
| Length of a section | One block of about 900 words | Four subsections of about 200 words each |
| Navigation | None; read it all | Headings that name each organelle group |
| Reuse | Take it all or nothing | A teacher can lift one subsection |

Other signals help too. A topic sentence at the start of each paragraph tells readers what it is about. Transitions such as "next" and "by contrast" tell them how ideas connect. Lists and tables show parallel items at a glance. And a short summary at the end of a section gives readers a chance to check their understanding before moving on.

**Headings and subheadings** are the labels that name a section and its parts. They work as a map of the text, and screen-reader users depend on them to jump around, so their levels should follow in order without skipping, a point Chapter 7 returns to. A good heading says what the section is about in a few words, and a series of headings read alone should outline the chapter.

**Audience fit** is the overall match between a resource and the readers it is meant for, taking in reading level, prerequisite knowledge, and the context of use. It is the sum of this metric's evidence and Alignment's. For Sam's ninth graders, the open chapter fits partly: its content matches two of the three outcomes, its reading level is 1.5 grades high, and it assumes knowledge of lipids and proteins that the students do not yet have. None of those problems is fatal, and every one can be repaired because the license permits it. That is the point of reviewing a resource before adopting it: you learn what the repair list will be.

!!! mascot-tip "Sample the Whole Chapter"
    ![Echo the Loon giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Don't calculate reading level from the friendly first page alone. Sample one passage from the beginning, the middle, and the end, because chapters often get harder as they go.

## Summary

This chapter showed how to score the two fit metrics and what evidence supports each. The key points are:

- A learning outcome states what a learner will be able to do, and a learning objective is the narrower version for one lesson, written with a measurable verb.
- Bloom's Taxonomy sorts goals into six cognitive levels, which lets a reviewer check whether a resource asks for the thinking an outcome requires.
- The alignment metric uses a matrix of outcomes against the resource's coverage, and it surfaces content gaps, outcome coverage, and assessment mismatches.
- Standards alignment, a curriculum map, scope and sequence, and prerequisite knowledge place a resource in the context of a course.
- The readability metric rests on reading level, vocabulary, and text structure, and the Flesch-Kincaid formula estimates a grade level from sentence length and word length.
- Defined terms, a glossary, plain language, and fewer jargon words help the intended audience, and headings make a text easy to navigate.
- Audience fit combines reading level, prerequisite knowledge, and context, and a mismatch shows the repair list.

    ![Echo the Loon celebrating](../../img/mascot/celebration.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    You can now line a resource up against your outcomes, name its content gaps, and measure its reading level against your audience. Chapter 7 turns to a quality metric that decides whether every learner can use a resource at all: accessibility.
