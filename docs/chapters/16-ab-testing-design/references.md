# References: Designing an A/B Test with Students

1. [A/B testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A/B_testing) - Wikipedia - Defines A/B testing, how users are split between versions, and how results are compared. The core idea behind comparing an original resource with its improved version.

2. [Randomized controlled trial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_controlled_trial) - Wikipedia - Explains random assignment to treatment and control groups and why it supports causal claims. Background for the chapter's sections on random assignment and control and treatment groups.

3. [Informed consent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Informed_consent) - Wikipedia - Describes what people must be told and agree to before taking part in research, and the special rules for minors. Supports the chapter's guidance on consent and student privacy.

4. Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: A Practical Guide to A/B Testing - Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang, and Ya Xu - Cambridge University Press (2020) - Drawing on years of running experiments at large technology companies, the authors explain Twyman's law and sample ratio mismatch as checks that catch broken experiments before anyone trusts the result.

5. Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Generalized Causal Inference - William R. Shadish, Thomas D. Cook, and Donald T. Campbell - Houghton Mifflin (2002) - Extends Campbell and Stanley's 1963 list of threats to validity, such as history, maturation, and selection. That list remains the standard vocabulary for why two groups differ for reasons other than the treatment.

6. [Sample Size Calculator](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html) - Evan Miller - A free calculator that estimates how many participants a two-group test needs for a chosen effect and confidence. Supports the chapter's sample size planning.

7. [How Not To Run an A/B Test](https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-run-an-ab-test.html) - Evan Miller - Explains why checking results repeatedly and stopping when they look good inflates false positives. A caution for anyone running a small test with students.

8. [Identifying and Implementing Educational Practices Supported by Rigorous Evidence](https://ies.ed.gov/use-work/resource-library/resource/handbook/identifying-and-implementing-educational-practices) - Institute of Education Sciences - A user-friendly guide to what counts as strong evidence in education, with emphasis on randomized studies. Helps teachers judge what their own small test can and cannot show.

9. [What Works Clearinghouse Handbooks](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Handbooks) - Institute of Education Sciences - The standards the WWC uses to rate study designs, covering random assignment, attrition, and baseline equivalence. A benchmark for a well-planned classroom comparison.

10. [Protection of Human Subjects (45 CFR Part 46)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-45/subtitle-A/subchapter-A/part-46) - eCFR - The federal regulations, known as the Common Rule, for research with human subjects, including informed-consent requirements. Context for the chapter's consent planning and when review boards are involved.
