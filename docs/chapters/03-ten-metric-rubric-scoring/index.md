## OER Quality

**OER quality** is the degree to which an open educational resource helps its intended learners reach its intended learning outcomes. Notice what the definition leaves out: how polished the resource looks, what awards it won, and what it cost. Quality describes a relationship between a resource and a learner. A resource that a graduate student finds excellent can be poor quality for a sixth grader, and the reverse is also true.

OER quality varies more than the quality of commercial textbooks, for reasons that are easy to see:

- **No editorial gate:** anyone can publish an OER, so no editor or review board need approve it.
- **Uneven funding and review:** some OER come from funded projects with peer review, and others from one teacher's spare evenings.
- **Age:** facts, links, and software versions go out of date, and a resource that was right five years ago may be wrong now.
- **Uneven skills:** an author who knows the subject may not know how to make a resource accessible to a blind learner.
- **Remixing:** each copy and revision can add errors or drop credit, and no one is required to check.

None of this makes OER worse than other materials. It means the label *open* tells you nothing about quality, which is why a reviewer needs a method. Quality also has parts. A resource can fail a learner in several different ways, and a single "good" or "bad" hides which one.

**Worked example: three resources, three blocked learners.** Each resource below has a strength and a failure, and the failure blocks a different learner.

| Resource | Its failure | Learner it blocks | Part of quality that failed |
|---|---|---|---|
| A chemistry reading with correct facts, written at a university level | Words and sentences are too hard | A grade 8 student who quits after two paragraphs | Readability |
| A well-written history unit that cites a 1990s source for current data | Figures are out of date | Students who memorize outdated numbers | Accuracy and currency |
| A clear science lab page whose diagrams have no text descriptions | Diagrams cannot be read by a screen reader | A blind student who cannot do the lab | Accessibility |

The same word, *quality*, covers all three failures. A star rating would give the three resources similar scores and hide what to fix.

Quality also depends on the learner. The same chemistry reading can be excellent for a first-year college class and poor for a grade 8 class, because the words, the pace, and the background it assumes fit one audience and not the other. A reviewer therefore starts every review by writing down the intended audience and the intended outcomes, and scores the resource against those. A resource that never states its audience or its outcomes has a quality problem before the review begins, because no one can say what it was built to do.

## Quality Rubric

A **rubric** is a scoring guide that lists the criteria for judging a piece of work and describes what each level of performance looks like. A **quality rubric** applies that idea to the quality of a resource. It replaces "this seems good" with named criteria, a scale, and rules about what counts as proof.

A quality rubric has five parts:

- **Criteria:** the specific things to be judged, such as accuracy or accessibility.
- **A scale:** the numbers a reviewer may assign.
- **Descriptors:** short statements of what each score level looks like.
- **Evidence rules:** what a reviewer must record to support a score.
- **A way to combine scores:** how single scores become a summary and a decision.

Rubrics come in two broad kinds. A **holistic** rubric gives one overall rating for a piece of work, such as "excellent" or "four stars". An **analytic** rubric scores several criteria separately and then combines them. Holistic rubrics are fast, but they hide what to fix and are hard to compare between reviewers. Analytic rubrics take longer and repay the time with detail, which is why this book uses one.

A rubric helps in four ways. It makes two reviewers look at the same things. It makes a score explainable, because the criteria are visible. It points to what to improve, because a low score names a specific metric. And it makes a decision defensible to a department or a district. Published rubrics do not always supply these parts. A 2015 review of 14 OER rubrics by Yuan and Recker, summarized in this book's [rubric specification](../../rubric-spec.md), reported that 5 of the 14 gave no rating scale and only 3 offered a detailed scoring guide.

**Worked example: two reviews of one resource.** A teacher reviews the reading *Watershed Basics*, the CC BY resource from Chapter 2, and a colleague reviews it with a rubric. The table shows what each review tells the next teacher.

| | Unstructured review | Rubric review |
|---|---|---|
| What it says | "Looks good. Clear and useful. Four stars." | Ten metrics, each scored from 0 to 10, each with evidence |
| What the next teacher learns | That one person liked it | That facts are mostly current, the video has no captions, and the metadata is nearly empty |
| Can another reviewer repeat it? | No: there is nothing to check | Yes: each score names where to look |
| What to fix first | Unknown | The lowest-scoring metrics |

## Ten-Metric Rubric

The **ten-metric rubric** is the quality rubric this book uses. It scores a resource on ten metrics. Each metric is worth 10 points, five metrics measure quality, five measure reusability, and the ten scores add up to a total of 100. Reviewers score each metric separately and record evidence for each score. The table lists the ten metrics in the order the worksheet uses, with the question each one answers and the chapter that teaches it.

| # | Metric | The question it answers | Half | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Accuracy and currency | Are the facts correct and up to date? | Quality | 5 |
| 2 | Alignment | Do the activities and tests match the stated learning outcomes? | Quality | 6 |
| 3 | Readability | Do the words fit the audience? | Quality | 6 |
| 4 | Accessibility | Can learners with disabilities use it? | Quality | 7 |
| 5 | Practice with feedback | Can learners try things and learn how they did? | Quality | 8 |
| 6 | Open license | Does a clear license say what others may do? | Reusability | 9 |
| 7 | Modularity | Can each part stand alone? | Reusability | 9 |
| 8 | Metadata | Can others find it and tell what it is? | Reusability | 9 |
| 9 | Editable source | Can others change it? | Reusability | 4 |
| 10 | Portability | Does it work in other places without a login? | Reusability | 10 |

The numbers show the order on the worksheet and do not rank importance, because every metric carries the same 10 points. Ten is a compromise. One review of published rubrics found indicator counts ranging from 3 to 42, and a long rubric costs reviewers time that they stop spending. Ten metrics cover both halves of a resource and stay short enough for a careful reviewer to finish.

The rubric also leaves things out on purpose. It does not measure whether students learn more from the resource, because a rubric looks at the resource and not at its results. It does not measure fit with your particular students, your school's schedule, or your budget. And it does not replace your professional judgment about what to teach. Chapters 16 and 17 test the one claim a rubric cannot make for itself, which is that higher-scoring resources help learners more.

### Rubric Metric

A **rubric metric** is one measurable aspect of a resource that the rubric scores. Every metric in this book has the same five parts: a name, a question, a list of what to check, the score scale, and the rules for evidence. Keeping the parts the same lets a reviewer move from one metric to the next without learning a new procedure. A metric also measures one thing. A metric that mixes two ideas, such as "clear and accessible", cannot be scored, because a resource can be clear and inaccessible at once.

**Worked example: taking apart readability.** The table shows the five parts for the readability metric.

| Part | Readability |
|---|---|
| Name | Readability |
| Question | Do the words fit the audience? |
| What to check | The reading level fits the audience, and terms are defined before use. |
| Scale | Whole numbers from 0 to 10. |
| Evidence rule | Record the measured reading level of sampled passages and list any terms used before they are defined. |

Every metric is scored on its own, and the ten scores are never averaged into a single judgment before they are recorded. Chapters 4 through 10 give the what-to-check list and the evidence rule for each of the ten.

Metrics fail in predictable ways, and a good one avoids them. The table shows four common problems, with a weak metric and a better one for each.

| Problem | Weak metric | Better metric |
|---|---|---|
| Two ideas in one | "Clear and accessible" | Readability and accessibility, as two separate metrics |
| No way to check | "Is engaging" | Practice with feedback: count the practice items and the feedback messages |
| Vague words | "Has good images" | Accessibility: every image has a useful text description |
| Judges the author | "Written by an expert" | Accuracy and currency: facts match the cited sources |

Every metric in this rubric is also worth the same 10 points. Equal weights are a choice. Some reviewers will feel that accuracy matters more than metadata, and in a classroom decision it often does. The rubric keeps the weights equal so that scores are easy to compare between resources and reviewers, and it protects the most important metrics with a floor in the adoption threshold, described later in this chapter.

### Quality Dimension

A **quality dimension** is a group of rubric metrics that measure one side of a resource. The rubric has two dimensions, and the quality dimension is the group of five metrics that measure how well the resource helps learners learn. Its subtotal is worth 50 points.

- **Accuracy and currency** asks whether the content is correct and up to date. A learner who studies a wrong fact has learned something false.
- **Alignment** asks whether the activities and tests match the stated learning outcomes. A resource can teach well and still fail a course if it teaches the wrong thing.
- **Readability** asks whether the wording fits the audience, including whether terms are defined before they are used.
- **Accessibility** asks whether learners with disabilities can use the resource, following the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 at level AA.
- **Practice with feedback** asks whether learners can try something and learn how they did, through quizzes, worked examples, or interactive simulations.

**Worked example: five learners, five metrics.** Each learner in the table below meets a quality failure, and each failure belongs to a different metric.

| Learner | What goes wrong | Metric that would catch it |
|---|---|---|
| A student relies on a figure from an old source | The figure is out of date | Accuracy and currency |
| A teacher builds a unit around the resource | The test measures skills the reading never teaches | Alignment |
| A grade 6 student reads the passage | The vocabulary is far above grade level | Readability |
| A student with low vision enlarges the page | Text is part of an image and cannot be enlarged | Accessibility |
| A student finishes the chapter | There is nothing to try and no way to check understanding | Practice with feedback |

The five quality metrics do not overlap. Each picks out a way a learner can be let down, so a resource must be checked against all five before anyone calls it good.

The five metrics do touch each other. Readability and accessibility both depend on plain, well-structured writing, and alignment and practice both depend on clear learning outcomes, so a weak outcome statement can lower two scores at once. The metrics stay separate because each asks a different question and has a different fix: a readability problem is fixed by rewriting, and an accessibility problem by adding descriptions, captions, and structure.

### Reusability Dimension

A **reusability dimension** is the second group of rubric metrics, and it measures how easily other people can find, change, and reuse the resource. It also has five metrics and a 50-point subtotal.

- **Open license** asks whether a clear license states what others may do and whether attribution is complete.
- **Modularity** asks whether each section, quiz, or simulation stands alone and can be embedded elsewhere.
- **Metadata** asks whether the title, author, subject, grade level, license, and description are recorded so that others can find the resource and know what it is.
- **Editable source** asks whether the source is in a format others can change, such as Markdown or HTML, and not only a locked PDF.
- **Portability** asks whether the resource runs in a browser with no login and can be linked or embedded elsewhere.

These five connect to the 5R permissions from Chapter 1. A license grants the permissions, and the other four metrics decide whether you can use them in practice. An editable source makes revising and remixing possible. Modularity makes it possible to reuse one part without taking the rest. Metadata helps others find the work and credit it. Portability lets the work move to a new platform for use or redistribution.

**Worked example: five obstacles.** A teacher tries to reuse a chapter that scores poorly on reusability. Each obstacle she meets maps to one metric.

| What the teacher tries | What stops her | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| She looks for the license | No license is stated anywhere | Open license |
| She wants one quiz from the chapter | The quiz is woven into the chapter text | Modularity |
| She searches for the chapter by topic | Nothing records its subject or grade level | Metadata |
| She tries to change an example | Only a PDF is available | Editable source |
| She embeds the chapter in her course | The page asks visitors to log in | Portability |

The five reusability metrics depend on each other in a rough order. A reader cannot reuse what they cannot find, so metadata comes first. They cannot reuse what they may not use, so the license comes next. Then the work has to run in the new place (portability), be separable (modularity), and be changeable (editable source). A resource that fails an early step makes the later ones moot: the best-structured module in the world is no use if no one can find it or know whether they may use it.

### Quality Versus Reusability

**Quality versus reusability** names the idea that the two halves of the rubric are independent. A resource can be strong on one half and weak on the other, and any of the four combinations occurs in practice. The table shows the four combinations and where to spend effort.

| | Reusability high | Reusability low |
|---|---|---|
| **Quality high** | Strong and open: adopt it | Good but locked: improve license, metadata, source, or portability |
| **Quality low** | Open but weak: improve accuracy, readability, accessibility, or practice | Weak and locked: look elsewhere |

## Scoring One Metric

Each metric is scored in two steps. First the reviewer chooses a number using the scale and the descriptors. Second the reviewer records the evidence that justifies the number. This section covers the first step, and the section after it covers the second.

### Score Scale

The **score scale** is the range of numbers a reviewer may assign to a metric. In this book the scale runs from 0 to 10 in whole numbers. A 0 means the resource fails the check completely. A 10 means the resource meets the check fully and the reviewer found no gaps. Whole numbers are a deliberate choice, because half points suggest a precision that human reviewers cannot deliver and invite arguments that carry no information. Every metric uses the same scale, so a 6 for readability and a 6 for portability mean the same thing: roughly half to three quarters of what the check asks is in place.

### Scoring Descriptor

A **scoring descriptor** is a short statement that tells a reviewer what one level of the scale looks like. Without descriptors, one reviewer's 7 is another reviewer's 9, and the numbers cannot be compared. The table gives the descriptors used for every metric, along with the approximate share of the metric's check that is met at each level. For a metric with ten separate checks, five passed checks is a share of 50 percent. Chapters 5 through 10 supply the actual checks for each metric.

| Score | Descriptor | What it looks like | Share of the check met |

### Metric Score

A **metric score** is the whole number from 0 to 10 that a reviewer assigns to one metric of one resource. It is the unit that everything later in the chapter builds on: subtotals, totals, comparisons, and improvement plans are all made from metric scores. Four rules keep them honest:

- **Score what you checked.** If you did not test the embed, you cannot score portability.
- **Score the resource as it is today.** Do not credit a fix you plan to make or an improvement the author promises.
- **Score each metric by itself.** A strong score on one metric must not lift another.
- **Record a number for every metric.** A skipped metric leaves a hole in the total.

To choose the number, count the checks the resource meets, find the band that holds that share, and then pick within the band. If the band has two numbers, choose the lower one when a gap stops a learner or a reuser from doing something the check protects, and the higher one when the gaps are cosmetic. Give a 10 only when every check passed and you looked everywhere the check applies.

**Worked example: scoring open license for Watershed Basics.** The reviewer applies ten illustrative checks to the resource. Chapter 9 gives the real list.

| # | Check | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A license is named on the main page | Pass |
| 2 | The license version is stated | Pass |
| 3 | A link to the license text is given | Pass |
| 4 | The owner is named | Pass |
| 5 | The scope of the license is stated | Pass |
| 6 | The license appears on every page (6 of 6 pages) | Pass |
| 7 | Machine-readable license marking is present | Pass |
| 8 | Third-party image 1 carries its own notice | Pass |
| 9 | Third-party image 2 carries its own notice | Pass |
| 10 | Third-party image 3 carries its own notice | Fail |

Nine of ten checks pass, a share of 90 percent, which falls in the 9 to 10 band. A gap exists, and it matters: nothing tells a reuser whether image 3 may be copied. The reviewer therefore chooses the lower number, **9**, and records the evidence for it.

A second case shows the choice from the other side. Two reviewers each find that 6 of 10 readability checks pass, a share of 60 percent, in the 5 to 6 band. The first notes that the failed checks are all small wording choices and chooses 6. The second finds that a technical term the reader needs is never defined, and chooses 5, because that gap can stop a learner. Both followed the rule, and the evidence each recorded explains the one-point difference.

## Evidence for a Score

**Evidence for a score** is the specific, checkable observation that supports a metric score. Good evidence has three parts: where you looked, such as a page, a section, a URL, or a file; what you saw, such as a quote, a count, or a measurement; and the rule you compared it with, which is the check from the metric. With those three parts another reviewer can repeat your check and reach the same observation.

Evidence matters for four reasons:

- **Repeatability:** a second reviewer can verify a claim that names where to look.
- **Discussion:** when two scores differ, evidence is what the reviewers examine, and opinions are not.
- **Improvement:** an author can fix a problem that a score points to, but cannot fix "6 out of 10".
- **Checking AI agents:** [Chapter 12](../12-ai-agents-prompts-scoring/index.md) requires an agent to return evidence with every score, and [Chapter 14](../14-checking-agent-output/index.md) teaches you to test that evidence before you trust it.

Evidence takes a few common forms: a count (5 of 8 quiz questions give feedback), a quote (the sentence that contains the error), a measurement (a reading-level number from a tool), a test result (the embed failed on a named platform on a named date), or a screenshot. When a resource is too large to check in full, check a sample, and record how you chose it and how large it was. "I checked pages 2, 5, and 9" is evidence, and "I checked a few pages" is not.

An opinion is not evidence, and neither is a claim by an AI agent that nobody has checked. The table shows weak and strong evidence for four metrics.

| Metric | Weak: an opinion | Strong: checkable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy and currency | "The facts seem right." | "Checked 10 facts in sections 1 to 3 against the cited sources. Nine matched. The river length in section 2 is outdated." |
| Accessibility | "The alt text is probably fine." | "4 of 5 images have alt text, and 2 of those 4 are one word long ('photo')." |
| Open license | "I think it is CC BY." | "The footer of 6 of 6 pages says CC BY 4.0 and links to the license." |
| Portability | "It works fine." | "Opened in 3 browsers with no login. The iframe embed worked on 2 of 3 test platforms and failed on 1, tested 2026-10-12." |

!!! mascot-thinking "No Evidence, No Score"
    ![Echo thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A score without evidence is a guess with a number attached. If you cannot point to where you looked, you have not scored the metric yet.

### Evidence Citation

An **evidence citation** is a short written record of one piece of evidence, in a form that lets another reviewer find it in a minute. The simplest form has three parts, in this order: where, what, and rule. Add a date for anything that can change, such as a link test or a tool report, because a link that works today may fail next month.

**Worked example: two citations.** The first citation supports an accuracy score. The second supports a link-currency finding.

| Where | What | Rule | Date |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section 2, paragraph 3 | The text gives a river's population as 2 million, and the cited 2010 source gives 1.6 million. | Facts match the cited sources. | Not needed: the text will not change by itself. |
| Pages 3 and 7 | The links to the water-quality data return a "page not found" error. | Links work. | 2026-10-12 |

!!! mascot-tip "Write Evidence While You Score"
    ![Echo giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }

## Adding Up the Scores

Ten metric scores are a lot to hold in mind. The rubric combines them in layers: first a subtotal for each half, then a total for the resource, then a band that turns the total into words, and finally a threshold that turns it into a decision. Each layer loses some detail, so the sections below also say what to report beside each number.

### Quality Subtotal

The **quality subtotal** is the sum of the five quality metric scores, so it ranges from 0 to 50. It answers one question: how well does this resource help learners reach the outcomes? To compute it, add the scores for accuracy and currency, alignment, readability, accessibility, and practice with feedback.

**Worked example: the quality subtotal for Watershed Basics.** The scores come from the full worksheet later in this chapter.

| Quality metric | Score |
|---|---|
| Accuracy and currency | 7 |
| Alignment | 6 |
| Readability | 8 |
| Accessibility | 5 |
| Practice with feedback | 6 |
| **Quality subtotal** | **32 of 50** |

A subtotal shows where the half stands but not which metrics are weak. The next table shows two resources with the same quality subtotal and very different problems.

| Resource | Accuracy | Alignment | Readability | Accessibility | Practice | Subtotal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 6 | 6 | 7 | 6 | 7 | 32 |
| Y | 10 | 10 | 10 | 2 | 0 | 32 |

Resource X is mediocre everywhere. Resource Y is excellent in three metrics and nearly closed to learners who use a screen reader, with no practice at all. The same subtotal calls for different action. Report the lowest metrics beside the subtotal, and never report the subtotal alone.

The subtotal is also a planning tool, because it shows what each point costs. Raising the lowest metric usually buys more than polishing a strong one. Suppose the *Watershed Basics* reviewers add captions to the video, fix the heading jump, and test color contrast, which could lift accessibility from 5 to 8. The quality subtotal rises from 32 to 35 for an afternoon of work. Raising readability from 8 to 9 would add one point for much more effort. A subtotal also describes the unit you reviewed. The worksheet says whether it covers a whole book, one chapter, or one quiz, and subtotals from different units should not be compared as if they were the same thing.

### Reusability Subtotal

The **reusability subtotal** is the sum of the five reusability metric scores, from 0 to 50. It answers a different question: how easily can other people find, change, and reuse this resource? Add the scores for open license, modularity, metadata, editable source, and portability.

**Worked example: the reusability subtotal for Watershed Basics.**

| Reusability metric | Score |
|---|---|
| Open license | 9 |
| Modularity | 5 |
| Metadata | 4 |
| Editable source | 8 |
| Portability | 7 |
| **Reusability subtotal** | **33 of 50** |

Some reusability metrics act as gates. A resource with an open-license score of 2 or less is not reusable in practice, because no one knows what they may do, whatever its other scores. A resource with no metadata cannot be found. A subtotal averages away these differences, so read the lowest metric before you read the sum. For *Watershed Basics* the lowest is metadata, at 4, and it is also the cheapest to fix: adding a subject, grade level, and description takes minutes, while restructuring a page to raise modularity takes much longer. [Chapter 15](../15-improving-resources-with-ai/index.md) turns this observation into an improvement plan.

Reusability scores often change quickly with small actions. Adding a license line to every page, filling in the metadata fields, and publishing the Markdown source can each move a metric by several points in an hour or two. Quality improvements usually take longer. That difference is useful when you plan a review cycle: fix the reusability gaps first, and the resource becomes shareable while you work on the harder quality fixes. The reverse order is a trap, because a polished resource that no one may reuse stays locked.

### Total Score

The **total score** is the sum of all ten metric scores, which is also the quality subtotal plus the reusability subtotal. It ranges from 0 to 100. It is the number most people will ask for, and it is the least informative of the numbers in a report, because it hides both the split between the halves and the weakest metric.

**Worked example: three resources, one total.** The table shows three resources that each total 65.

| Resource | Quality subtotal | Reusability subtotal | Total | Lowest metric | What it needs first |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Watershed Basics | 32 | 33 | 65 | Metadata, 4 | Fill in the metadata, then fix accessibility |
| Locked Chemistry E-book | 44 | 21 | 65 | Open license, 1 | A license, or a different resource |
| Open Unit With Errors | 28 | 37 | 65 | Accuracy and currency, 3 | A fact check before anything else |

Three resources with the same total need three different actions. Report the total together with the two subtotals and the lowest metric.

Totals suit one job especially well: comparing two versions of the same resource before and after improvement. A rise from 65 to 78 after a round of fixes is a meaningful signal. Totals are weaker for ranking different resources, because the resources may differ in the unit reviewed, the reviewers, and the evidence behind each score. A difference of a point or two between totals is within normal reviewer wobble, so do not rank on it. When you compare resources, compare the subtotals and the lowest metrics too, and say who reviewed each one.

!!! mascot-warning "A Total Can Hide a Failed Metric"
    ![Echo warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A resource with an open-license score of 1 and nine strong scores can still total 80, and no one can lawfully reuse it. Read the lowest metric before you read the total, and report both.

### Score Band

A **score band** is a named range of total scores that turns a number into a plain word. The bands give readers a quick sense of where a resource stands, and they do not replace the metric scores. The ranges include both end values.

| Band | Total score | What it suggests |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 90 to 100 | Ready to use, with only small improvements to make |
| Good | 75 to 89 | Usable, with the weak metrics noted |
| Fair | 50 to 74 | Needs revision before use |
| Weak | 25 to 49 | Needs heavy revision, or look elsewhere |
| Poor | 0 to 24 | Not worth reviewing further |

### Adoption Threshold

An **adoption threshold** is the rule that turns a score into a decision. This book's starting rule has two parts, because a total alone can hide a failed metric. The resource is ready to adopt when its total is at least 75 **and** every metric scores at least 5. The second part is a floor that stops one failed metric from hiding inside a high total.

| Decision | Rule |
|---|---|
| Adopt | Total >= 75, and every metric >= 5 |
| Revise | Total >= 50, and the Adopt rule is not met |
| Reject | Total < 50 |

These values are starting points for this book and not a standard. A department may set stricter ones. [Chapter 11](../11-manual-evaluation-adoption/index.md) shows how to apply the rule, and [Chapter 16](../16-ab-testing-design/index.md) and [Chapter 17](../17-ab-testing-analysis/index.md) test whether rubric scores predict learning.

## Rubric Worksheet

The **rubric worksheet** is the form on which a reviewer records everything about one review: the resource's identity, the ten metric scores, the evidence for each score, the subtotals, and the total. This book's [sample rubric worksheet](../../workshops/sample-rubric-worksheet.md) is one version of it. The worksheet has a resource block at the top and two tables of five rows each, one for quality and one for reusability. Each row has the metric name, the question to check, a column for your score, a column for an AI agent's score, and a column for evidence. A totals box sits at the bottom. The agent column stays blank in a manual review, and Chapter 12 puts it to use.

A review with the worksheet follows six steps:

1. Fill in the resource block, including the date you accessed the resource.
2. Read the what-to-check text for a metric before you open the resource.
3. Examine the resource, collecting evidence as you go.
4. Choose the metric score, using the descriptors.
5. Write the evidence citation in the same row.
6. After all ten rows, compute the two subtotals and the total, then read the lowest metric.

**Worked example: the completed worksheet for Watershed Basics.** The resource is the CC BY 4.0 reading by Maria Lopez from Chapter 2, hosted as a web page with a quiz. It is an illustrative case, and the evidence is written the way a reviewer would write it.

| Metric | Score | Evidence |

### Review Checklist

A **review checklist** is a short list of things to gather before you start scoring, so that you do not stop halfway to look for a missing piece. A useful checklist has seven items:

- The resource's address or file, and the date you accessed it.
- The license, if one is stated.
- The intended audience and the stated learning outcomes.
- Access to the editable source, if it exists.
- The tools you will use: a readability checker, an accessibility checker, and a link checker.
- A second reviewer, if you can arrange one.
- A quiet block of time to review the whole resource, not a fragment.

!!! mascot-tip "Save a Dated Copy"
    ![Echo giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Save a copy or a screenshot of the version you scored, with the date. A web page can change the next day, and your evidence will not match what the next reviewer sees.

### Review Report

A **review report** is the finished document that a review produces. It contains the completed worksheet, the two subtotals and the total, the band and the decision, and the three improvements that would raise the score most. It also names the reviewer and the date, and it lists any open questions, such as a metric that needs a second opinion. A report that gives only the total is not a report. Readers need the evidence, because evidence is what lets them agree, disagree, or act.

## Reviewer

A **reviewer** is the person, or the AI agent, who applies the rubric to a resource and records scores and evidence. Most human reviewers are teachers, instructional designers, librarians, or OER coordinators. A reviewer needs access to subject knowledge for the accuracy and alignment metrics, the ability to run an accessibility checker, and basic comfort with web pages and files. A reviewer without subject knowledge can still score most metrics, but should ask a subject expert about accuracy. [Chapter 12](../12-ai-agents-prompts-scoring/index.md) introduces AI agents as reviewers. An agent's scores are always checked by a human, and [Chapter 14](../14-checking-agent-output/index.md) shows how.

The table below lists what each metric asks of a reviewer. No one person needs every skill, which is another reason to review in pairs.

| Metric | What the reviewer needs |
|---|---|
| Accuracy and currency | Subject knowledge, or a subject expert to ask, and the ability to follow citations |
| Alignment | The course outcomes and a working knowledge of Bloom's taxonomy |
| Readability | A readability tool and a sense of the audience |
| Accessibility | An accessibility checker, a screen reader to try, and the WCAG 2.1 AA checks |
| Practice with feedback | Experience writing and judging quiz questions |
| Open license | The ability to read a license statement, as in Chapter 2 |
| Modularity | Willingness to take the resource apart |
| Metadata | Knowledge of the fields to expect |
| Editable source | The ability to open the source files |
| Portability | Access to a few browsers and a test platform |

The author of a resource should not be its only reviewer, because authors see what they meant and not what is on the page. A reviewer with a stake in the outcome, such as a vendor or the author's close collaborator, should say so in the report. Disclosing the relationship does not disqualify the review, and it lets readers weigh it.

A review works best with two reviewers, one who leads and one who scores independently. The process has five steps. Both reviewers read the checklist and the resource. Each scores alone. They compare scores metric by metric. They discuss only the metrics where scores differ by more than 3 points, using their evidence. The lead then completes the report. Scoring alone first matters, because a reviewer who sees another score first tends to move toward it.

**Worked example: a two-reviewer review.** Two teachers review *Watershed Basics* over three days.

| Day | Who | What they do | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Both | Read the checklist, gather tools, and read the resource | Notes only, no scores |
| 1 to 2 | Each, alone | Score all ten metrics with evidence | Two separate worksheets |
| 3 | Both | Compare the worksheets metric by metric | A list of metrics that differ by more than 3 points |
| 3 | Both | Discuss each flagged metric, showing evidence | An agreed score or a recorded disagreement for each |
| 3 | Lead | Complete the report | One review report |

### Reviewer Bias

**Reviewer bias** is a systematic tendency in a reviewer's judgment that comes from the reviewer and not from the resource. Biases are not character flaws. Everyone has them, and a method works by catching them. The table lists six common biases and the defense against each.

| Bias | What happens | Defense |
|---|---|---|
| Halo effect | One striking strength lifts every score | Score one metric at a time, across the whole resource |
| Anchoring | The first number you see or write pulls the later ones toward it | Score alone, and do not see others' scores first |
| Leniency | You score generously because the author worked hard or the resource is free | Score against the descriptors, not against the effort |
| Author or brand bias | A famous author or publisher makes you skip checks | Run every check, whatever the source |
| Confirmation bias | You expect a result and stop looking when you find it | Look for evidence that would change the score |
| Fatigue | Late metrics get less careful checks | Take breaks, and review in two sessions |

!!! mascot-warning "Score Alone Before You Compare"
    ![Echo warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A number you see first pulls your judgment toward it, and you will not feel it happen. Finish your own ten scores, with evidence, before you look at a colleague's or an agent's.

## Inter-Rater Agreement

**Inter-rater agreement** is how closely the scores of two or more reviewers match for the same resource. It tells you whether the rubric is being applied consistently. If two reviewers give very different scores, either the rubric is unclear or the reviewers need calibration. This book uses a simple measure. A metric **agrees** when the two scores differ by 3 points or fewer, and it is **flagged** when they differ by more than 3. The percent agreement is the number of agreeing metrics divided by 10, times 100.

Compare reviewers metric by metric, because two reviewers can give the same total for opposite reasons. [Chapter 14](../14-checking-agent-output/index.md) adds a statistic that corrects for agreement by chance. For now the simple count is enough to find where a conversation is needed.

**Worked example: two reviewers, one resource.** Reviewer 1 is the worksheet from the previous section. Reviewer 2 scored the same resource alone.

| Metric | Reviewer 1 | Reviewer 2 | Difference | Agrees? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy and currency | 7 | 8 | 1 | Yes |
| Alignment | 6 | 4 | 2 | Yes |
| Readability | 8 | 8 | 0 | Yes |
| Accessibility | 5 | 9 | 4 | **Flagged** |
| Practice with feedback | 6 | 6 | 0 | Yes |
| Open license | 9 | 9 | 0 | Yes |
| Modularity | 5 | 7 | 2 | Yes |

| Editable source | 8 | 8 | 0 | Yes |
| Portability | 7 | 3 | 4 | **Flagged** |
| **Total** | **65** | **65** | **0** | |

Eight of the ten metrics agree, so the percent agreement is 80 percent. Two metrics are flagged: accessibility and portability. The two totals are identical, at 65, yet the reviewers disagree sharply on two metrics, and the equal totals hide it. The reviewers would now compare their evidence for accessibility and portability. Reviewer 2 may have missed the video without captions, or Reviewer 1 may have missed a platform where the embed works.

## Trusting the Rubric

A rubric is a measuring tool, and a measuring tool can be wrong in two different ways. A scale that gives a different weight each time you step on it is unreliable. A scale that gives the same wrong weight every time is reliable but not valid. The next two sections name those two properties.

### Rubric Reliability

**Rubric reliability** is the consistency of a rubric's scores. A reliable rubric gives similar scores when different reviewers score the same resource, and when the same reviewer scores it again later. Inter-rater agreement measures the first kind. Re-scoring after a few weeks and comparing the two sets measures the second. Reliability improves when descriptors are clear, when checks are specific, and when reviewers practice together on sample resources before they review real ones. Reliability says nothing about whether the scores are right. A rubric can give the same wrong answer every time.

### Rubric Validity

**Rubric validity** is whether a rubric's scores measure what they claim to measure. For this rubric the claims are that the quality metrics reflect how well a resource helps learners and that the reusability metrics reflect how easily others can reuse it. Validity is checked in several ways. Experts judge whether the ten metrics cover what matters. Reviewers check whether resources that score high are in fact easier to reuse. And the strongest check asks whether students learn more from resources that score higher. Chapters 16 and 17 test that last claim with an A/B test, because agreeing on the wrong thing is always possible.

!!! mascot-thinking "Consistent Is Not Correct"
    ![Echo thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Two reviewers can agree perfectly on a rubric that misses what matters to learners. Reliability tells you the scale is steady, and validity tells you it is measuring the right thing.

## Key Takeaways

- **OER quality** is how well a resource helps its intended learners reach the intended outcomes, and the label *open* says nothing about it. A **quality rubric** replaces opinion with criteria, a scale, descriptors, evidence rules, and a way to combine scores.
- The **ten-metric rubric** has five quality metrics and five reusability metrics, each worth 10 points. **Quality versus reusability** means the two halves are independent, so keep two subtotals and read them side by side.
- A **metric score** is a whole number from 0 to 10 chosen with the **scoring descriptors**. **Evidence for a score** says where you looked, what you saw, and the rule you applied, and an **evidence citation** records it.
- The **quality subtotal** and **reusability subtotal** each run to 50, and the **total score** runs to 100. A **score band** names the range, and the **adoption threshold** turns the score into Adopt, Revise, or Reject with a floor for every metric.
- The **rubric worksheet** holds everything, a **review checklist** prepares you, and a **review report** presents the result.
- A **reviewer** scores alone first. **Reviewer bias** pulls scores off course, and **inter-rater agreement** shows where reviewers differ.
- **Rubric reliability** is consistency and **rubric validity** is whether the scores measure what matters. Both are needed.
