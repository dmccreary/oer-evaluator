## What Quality Means for an OER

**OER quality** is how well an open educational resource serves a particular learner and purpose, together with how easily other teachers can reuse it. The first half of that definition is about teaching. The second half is about reuse, and it is what separates OER from other materials, because the whole point of an open resource is that others can take it further. Chapter 1 noted that an open license says nothing about quality. This chapter turns the vague word "quality" into things you can check.

Before scoring anything, write down the context of the review. A **review context** is a short statement of who the resource is for and what it must do. Without one, the same resource can earn very different scores from reviewers who imagine different audiences. The table shows the context card for Sam's review, and every score in this book assumes a card like it has been filled in.

| Item | Sam's entry |
|------|-------------|
| Resource | "Cell Structure and Function," an open textbook chapter |
| Audience | Ninth-grade biology students, ages 14 to 15 |
| Learning outcomes | Identify the main organelles and their functions; compare plant and animal cells; explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves |
| Use | Assigned reading for a two-week unit, plus a set of practice questions |
| Constraints | Browsers on school devices only; some students use screen readers; some read below grade level |

Quality is never absolute. It depends on the audience and the goal. A college cell biology text can be an excellent resource for a college course and a poor one for a ninth-grade class, because its reading level and depth do not match the learners. The same resource can also be strong on one part of quality and weak on another, which is why one overall impression is not enough.

Sam's search turned up two open chapters on cell structure, and comparing them shows the point. The table sets each one against Sam's ninth-grade unit.

| Chapter | Strengths | Weaknesses | Fit for Sam's ninth graders |
|---------|-----------|------------|------------------------------|
| A college-level chapter | Accurate, complete, many references | Reading level far above ninth grade, no practice questions | Poor as it stands |
| A teacher-written chapter | Friendly, short sentences, good diagrams | Two factual errors, no license statement | Weak until the errors are fixed and the license is confirmed |

Neither chapter is simply "good" or "bad." Each is strong in some respects and weak in others, and the right question is which weaknesses matter for this audience and which can be repaired. A rubric gives that question a structure by splitting quality into groups of criteria.

The first group is the **quality dimension**: the five metrics that ask how well the resource teaches. A **metric** here means a single criterion that is scored on its own, and later in this chapter you will see exactly how. The five quality metrics ask whether the content is accurate and current, whether it aligns with the learning outcomes, whether learners can read it, whether people with different abilities can use it, and whether it gives learners practice with feedback. Each asks a question that a reviewer can answer by looking at the resource.

Here is how Sam can approach those five questions for the open chapter on cell structure, by looking for a first piece of evidence on each. The table lists the question, what a reviewer looks at, and one finding from Sam's first read.

| Quality metric | Question | What a reviewer looks at | Sam's first finding |
|----------------|----------|--------------------------|---------------------|
| Accuracy and Currency | Is it correct and up to date? | Facts, references, dates | The chapter says mitochondria are the only organelle with their own DNA, but chloroplasts have their own DNA too |
| Alignment | Does it match the learning outcomes? | Outcomes compared with the content | The unit asks students to compare plant and animal cells, and the chapter never compares them |
| Readability | Can the audience read it? | Sentence length, vocabulary, undefined terms | A term, phospholipid bilayer, is used four paragraphs before it is defined |
| Accessibility | Can everyone use it? | Alt text, headings, contrast, captions | The four diagrams have no alt text |
| Practice with Feedback | Does it let learners try and check? | Questions, answers, interactive pieces | Three end-of-chapter questions, with no answers |

The five metrics are ordered the way a learner meets a resource. The content has to be correct, it has to be the right content, the learner has to be able to read it, every learner has to be able to reach it, and the learner has to be able to do something with it. A failure early in that chain makes the later successes less useful: an accessible, well-practiced chapter that is wrong teaches the wrong thing well. That is why reviewers often check accuracy first.

The second group is the **reusability dimension**: the five metrics that ask how easily other teachers can reuse the resource. Reuse here is a measurable property, not a matter of opinion. The five reusability metrics ask whether the license is clear and open, whether the resource is split into standalone pieces, whether it is described well enough to be found, whether its source can be edited, and whether it works on other platforms. These metrics line up with the 5R permissions from Chapter 1, because each one protects a permission the license promises. A license grants Revise on paper, but if the only copy is a locked PDF, the permission cannot be used in practice.

Suppose a teacher in another district wants only Sam's section on the cell membrane for a slide deck. The license lets them take it. Without modularity, they must cut it out of a 6,000-word file by hand. Without metadata, they may never have found it. Without an editable source, they cannot change the examples, and without portability the embedded video will not play in their learning system. Each reusability metric removes one of those obstacles.

| Reusability metric | Question | The 5R permissions it protects |
|--------------------|----------|--------------------------------|
| Open License | Is the license clear, open, and credited correctly? | All five |
| Modularity | Can a piece be lifted out and used alone? | Reuse and Remix |
| Metadata | Is it described well enough to be found? | Supports finding and adopting before any of the five |
| Editable Source | Can it be edited in an open format? | Revise and Remix |
| Portability | Does it run elsewhere, without a login or lock-in? | Reuse and Redistribute |

Sam's chapter does well here on the license, which is CC BY 4.0, and less well on the others. It is one 6,000-word file, so a teacher cannot easily lift just the section on the cell membrane. Its description lists only a title and an author. These findings return in later chapters, where each metric gets a full chapter of its own.

## Rubrics and the Ten Metrics

A **quality rubric** is a scoring guide for judging a resource. It lists the criteria to judge and describes what each level of performance looks like. Teachers know rubrics from grading student work, and the idea is the same: instead of forming one overall impression, the reviewer scores each criterion separately against written descriptions.

Three benefits explain why rubrics are worth the effort. They make reviews consistent, because two reviewers read the same descriptions. They make reviews transparent, because anyone can see how a score was reached. And they make reviews comparable, because two resources scored on the same criteria can be set side by side.

A good rubric criterion has four properties. It is observable, meaning a reviewer can point at something. It is independent, so scoring it does not depend on scoring another. It is specific, naming what to look at. And it comes with a scale, so the reviewer knows what each number means. The table contrasts a weak criterion with a strong one for the same concern. The strong criteria are longer, and every extra word helps two reviewers agree.

| Concern | Weak criterion | Strong criterion |
|---------|----------------|------------------|
| Clear writing | "The text is good." | "Terms are defined before they are used, and sentences are short enough for the audience." |
| Access | "The resource is accessible." | "Each image has alt text, and each video has captions." |
| Practice | "There are enough questions." | "Each section ends with questions that give feedback on the learner's answer." |

Rubrics for OER already exist, and they vary in quality. A 2015 review by Min Yuan and Mimi Recker examined 14 rubrics for judging OER quality. It found that 5 of the 14 gave no rating scale and that only 3 offered a detailed scoring guide. A rubric without a scale or a scoring guide leaves each reviewer to invent their own standard, which defeats the purpose. You can read the review, which is published under a Creative Commons Attribution license, at [doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i5.2389](https://doi.org/10.19173/irrodl.v16i5.2389). This book's rubric addresses those two gaps by giving every metric a scale and written descriptors.

To see the difference a rubric makes, imagine Sam and a colleague reviewing the same chapter. Without a rubric, each writes a sentence or two, and the table shows how little the two sets of comments can be compared. With a rubric, they compare numbers metric by metric and discuss only the metrics where they differ.

| Question | Review without a rubric | Review with a rubric |
|----------|-------------------------|----------------------|
| Do two reviewers use the same standard? | Not necessarily | Yes, they use the same written descriptors |
| Can you see why a score was given? | Rarely | Yes, each score has recorded evidence |
| Can you compare two resources? | Only loosely | Yes, on the same ten metrics |
| Can you tell what to fix first? | Hard to say | Yes, the lowest-scoring metrics show it |

The **ten-metric rubric** is this book's rubric. It scores a resource on the ten metrics introduced above, five for quality and five for reusability, each on a scale of 0 to 10, for a maximum of 100 points. The table lists all ten, shows which dimension each belongs to, and says which chapter covers it.

| # | Metric | Dimension | What it asks | Chapter |
|---|--------|-----------|--------------|---------|
| 1 | Accuracy and Currency | Quality | Are the facts correct and current? | 5 |
| 2 | Alignment | Quality | Does it match the learning outcomes? | 6 |
| 3 | Readability | Quality | Does the reading level fit the audience? | 6 |
| 4 | Accessibility | Quality | Can learners with different abilities use it? | 7 |
| 5 | Practice with Feedback | Quality | Does it let learners practice and check? | 8 |
| 6 | Open License | Reusability | Is the license open and correctly credited? | 9 |
| 7 | Modularity | Reusability | Does it stand in standalone pieces? | 9 |
| 8 | Metadata | Reusability | Is it described so others can find it? | 9 |
| 9 | Editable Source | Reusability | Is the source in an editable, open format? | 4 |
| 10 | Portability | Reusability | Does it work on other platforms? | 10 |

Ten is a compromise. A shorter rubric leaves out things that matter, and a longer one tires reviewers into skimming. These ten cover the questions a teacher asks before adopting a resource, roughly in the order a resource's life runs: is it correct, does it fit, can learners read it, can everyone use it, does it let them practice, and can other teachers pick it up and carry it on. The rubric also has limits worth stating. It does not measure whether students actually learn from the resource, which only a test with students can show, and Chapters 16 and 17 return to that. It scores a resource against one audience and context, so a score earned for one group does not transfer to another. And it counts what can be checked, so some real strengths, such as a writer's warmth, go unscored.

## Scores, Scales, and Descriptors

A **metric score** is the whole number a reviewer assigns to one metric after examining the evidence. Four rules keep scores honest. The score is always a whole number from 0 to 10. It follows the evidence: a reviewer decides what was found, then picks the score, not the reverse. Only the parts that apply are scored: a resource with no video has no captions to check, so the reviewer judges the parts that do exist. And every score is recorded with the reason.

Take Sam scoring Accessibility on the open chapter. Sam lists what was found: the four diagrams have no alt text, text labels in the diagrams are pale gray on white, an eight-minute video has no captions, and the headings jump from level 2 to level 4. The page itself loads and the text can be read by a screen reader. Against the descriptors, most checks fail, so Sam assigns a score of 3. The list of findings, not Sam's impression, is what justifies the number. A second reviewer who finds the same four problems should land near the same score.

| Finding | Effect on the Accessibility score |
|---------|-----------------------------------|
| No alt text on four diagrams | Major deduction |
| Pale gray text on white in diagrams | Deduction |
| Video with no captions | Major deduction |
| Headings skip from level 2 to level 4 | Small deduction |
| Page text readable by a screen reader | Credit |

Moving from a list of findings to a number is the step reviewers find hardest, so it helps to follow the same four steps each time:

1. Read the descriptors for the metric and find the two ranges that best fit your findings.
2. Check the findings against the lower range's description. If most of them match it, the score belongs in the lower range.
3. Choose the number within the range by counting how many of the metric's checks pass. More passes mean a higher number in the range.
4. If you are torn between two numbers, choose the lower one, write down why, and flag the metric for your second reviewer.

For Sam's Accessibility findings, "most checks fail" matches the 1 to 3 range. One check passes and four fail, so Sam picked 3, the top of the range. Two edge cases come up often. If a metric does not apply to part of the resource, such as captions for a resource with no video, leave that part out and score the rest. And a score of 10 means the checks found no problems, not that the resource is flawless.

The **score scale** is the range and meaning of the numbers. This rubric uses whole numbers from 0 to 10, grouped into five ranges so that reviewers share a picture of what a number means.

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 0 | The criterion is absent or unmet |
| 1 to 3 | Serious problems; most checks fail |
| 4 to 6 | Partly meets the criterion; important gaps remain |
| 7 to 9 | Mostly meets the criterion; only minor gaps |
| 10 | Fully meets the criterion; no problems found |

A **scoring descriptor** is the written description of what a level looks like for one specific metric. Descriptors turn a number into observable facts, so that a 5 on Readability means the same thing to every reviewer. A good descriptor names things a reviewer can check. The table gives descriptors for three ranges of the Readability metric, using illustrative wording.

| Score range | Readability descriptor |
|-------------|------------------------|
| 9 to 10 | The reading level matches the audience, terms are defined before use, and sentences are short and direct |
| 5 to 6 | The reading level is one or two grades off, and some terms are used before they are defined |
| 1 to 3 | The reading level is far above the audience, and most technical terms are never defined |

## Evidence

**Evidence for a score** is a fact about the resource that another reviewer could check and that supports the score. Evidence has two parts: a location, which says where to look, and an observation, which says what is there. "Figure 2 has no alt text" is evidence. "The figures are hard to use" is an opinion, because a second reviewer cannot tell what to check.

Evidence matters for three reasons. When two reviewers disagree, they can settle it by looking at the same spot instead of arguing about impressions. Evidence also checks bias, because a reviewer who must name a specific observation cannot rely on a general feeling. And evidence tells the next teacher what to fix, since the problems themselves are the improvement list. The table shows the difference between opinion and evidence for three metrics.

| Metric | Opinion | Evidence |
|--------|---------|----------|
| Accuracy and Currency | The chapter seems accurate | Page 14 says mitochondria are the only organelle with their own DNA, but chloroplasts also have their own DNA |
| Accessibility | The images are not accessible | Figures 1 to 4 have no alt text, checked in the page source |
| Readability | It is hard to read | Section 2 uses "phospholipid bilayer" in its first paragraph and defines it in the fourth |

Sam's score of 6 for Accuracy and Currency rests on three pieces of evidence. The first is the mitochondria error. The second is a claim that ribosomes are found only in cells with a nucleus, which is wrong, because bacteria also have ribosomes. The third is that two of the nine links in the reference list no longer work. Everything else Sam checked was correct, so the score is not low, but each deduction points to something specific. Chapter 5 covers how to find such evidence.

Evidence comes in four kinds, and a strong review uses more than one. *Observed* evidence is something you can see, such as a missing label. *Measured* evidence comes from a tool or a calculation, such as a contrast ratio or a reading level. *Compared* evidence sets two things side by side, such as the learning outcomes against the chapter's contents. *Verified* evidence is checked against an outside source, such as looking up a fact. The table gives one example of each for Sam's chapter.

| Kind of evidence | Example from Sam's chapter |
|------------------|----------------------------|
| Observed | The video player has no caption button |
| Measured | The pale gray diagram labels measure about 2 to 1 against the white background (an illustrative figure) |
| Compared | The unit outcome "compare plant and animal cells" matches no section of the chapter |
| Verified | A reference text confirms that chloroplasts have their own DNA |

An **evidence citation** is the standard way to record evidence so that others can find it quickly. A good citation has three short parts. *Where* gives the location, such as a section, a figure number, or a page. *What* gives the observation. *How checked* says how the reviewer confirmed it, such as reading the page source, running a checker, or looking up a reference, and for web content it should include the date of the check. With those three parts, a second reviewer can find the item in under a minute. The table shows three citations for Sam's chapter.

| Where | What | How checked |
|-------|------|-------------|
| Section 3, Figure 4 | Diagram has no alt text | Read the page source on the review date |
| Section 2, paragraph 1 | "Phospholipid bilayer" used before it is defined | Read to the end of Section 2 |
| Reference list, items 3 and 7 | Links return "page not found" | Opened each link on the review date |

!!! mascot-tip "Where, What, How Checked"
    ![Echo the Loon giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Write each piece of evidence in three short parts: where you saw it, what you saw, and how you checked. If another reviewer can find it in under a minute, it's good evidence.

## Adding Up the Scores

Once each metric has a score, the scores are added. The **quality subtotal** is the sum of the five quality metric scores, so it ranges from 0 to 50. For Sam's chapter, the scores are 6 for Accuracy and Currency, 5 for Alignment, 5 for Readability, 3 for Accessibility, and 3 for Practice with Feedback. The quality subtotal is 6 + 5 + 5 + 3 + 3 = 22 out of 50.

A subtotal is a summary, and every summary hides something. Two resources can share a quality subtotal and still need very different repairs, as the table shows. Resource X is uniformly middling. Resource Y is excellent on two metrics and absent on the other three. Both score 25, but the second resource fails whole areas, and only the individual scores reveal that.

A subtotal is also fair only between resources of the same kind. A whole textbook and a single worksheet are scored on the same ten metrics, but a worksheet cannot show strong modularity the way a book of separate chapters can. Report the unit that was scored with every result, such as "the chapter," "the whole book," or "one component," and compare like with like.

| Resource | Accuracy | Alignment | Readability | Accessibility | Practice | Quality subtotal |
|----------|----------|-----------|-------------|---------------|----------|------------------|
| X | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 25 |
| Y | 10 | 10 | 5 | 0 | 0 | 25 |

The **reusability subtotal** is the sum of the five reusability metric scores, again from 0 to 50. Sam's scores are 10 for Open License, 5 for Modularity, 4 for Metadata, 8 for Editable Source, and 7 for Portability, so the subtotal is 10 + 5 + 4 + 8 + 7 = 34 out of 50. The table connects each score to the 5R permission it supports, which shows what a reuser can and cannot do.

| Metric | Sam's score | What it means for a teacher who wants to reuse the chapter |
|--------|-------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| Open License | 10 | The license is clear and open, so all five permissions are granted |
| Modularity | 5 | The chapter is one long file, so lifting out one section takes effort |
| Metadata | 4 | Only a title and an author are recorded, so it is hard to find by subject or level |
| Editable Source | 8 | An editable version exists, so the chapter can be revised and remixed |
| Portability | 7 | It runs in a browser, but the video depends on a streaming site |

One score deserves special attention. If a resource scores 0 on Open License, it is not an OER at all, and many teams treat that as a stop sign: they record the finding and do not go on to adopt the resource, whatever the other scores say.

Reusability scores also tend to improve faster than quality scores. Adding a metadata record, splitting a long file into sections, or exporting an editable copy can move three metrics in an afternoon, which makes reusability a good place to look for quick gains. Quality gains such as correcting facts or rewriting for a reading level take longer, because they need a subject expert's attention.

The **total score** is the quality subtotal plus the reusability subtotal, so it ranges from 0 to 100. Sam's chapter totals 22 + 34 = 56. The total is useful for ranking resources and for comparing a resource's score before and after improvement. The table gives Sam's complete scorecard, with every score and both subtotals.

Two cautions apply when you compare totals. A difference of a few points is within the noise of reviewer judgment, as the agreement example later in this chapter shows, so a sensible rule of thumb is to treat totals within about 5 points of each other as a tie. And report the date, because a score describes a resource as it stood on the day it was reviewed. An improved version deserves a new review.

!!! mascot-warning "A Total Can Hide a Failing Metric"

    A resource can reach a respectable total while one metric sits at zero, as Resource Y did above. Always read the individual scores and both subtotals before the total, and say so in your report.

A number alone does not say what to do. A **score band** is a range of total scores paired with a recommended action. The bands below are illustrative, and your school or team may set its own.

| Total score | Band | Recommended action |
|-------------|------|--------------------|
| 80 to 100 | Ready to adopt | Adopt, and note any small fixes |
| 60 to 79 | Adopt with improvements | Adopt after fixing the lowest-scoring metrics |
| 40 to 59 | Major revision needed | Improve substantially or choose another resource |
| 0 to 39 | Do not adopt | Choose another resource |

Sam's total of 56 puts the chapter in the "Major revision needed" band. That is useful to know: the chapter is open and has an editable source, so Sam can improve it, but it should not be handed to students as it stands.

Bands are also a way to describe change. After Sam improves the chapter, a new review might move it from "Major revision needed" to "Adopt with improvements." That jump, more than the exact number of points gained, is what Sam would report to the department.

An **adoption threshold** is the minimum a resource must reach before a team will adopt it. It often combines a minimum total with a floor for individual metrics. For example, a team might require a total of at least 70 and no metric below 3. Sam's chapter passes the floor but misses the total. Notice that Chapter 1's adoption routine now has a decision point at which the threshold applies.

!!! mascot-tip "Set the Threshold Before You Score"

    Decide your adoption threshold before you look at any scores. If you set it afterward, it's far too easy to place the line wherever your favorite resource lands.

The last idea in this section guards against a common mistake. **Quality versus reusability** is the distinction between the two dimensions, which are independent: a resource can be strong on one and weak on the other. The table sorts resources into four cases.

| | High reusability | Low reusability |
|---|------------------|-----------------|
| **High quality** | Ideal: adopt and share | Polished but locked: use only with permission, or look for an open alternative |
| **Low quality** | Open but weak: worth improving, because others can build on it | Skip, or start fresh |

Sam's chapter scored 22 of 50 on quality and 34 of 50 on reusability. That is low quality with fairly high reusability: the "open but weak" case. It is the best kind of weak, because the license and the editable source mean Sam, and any other teacher, can fix it. The polished publisher PDF in the calculator is the reverse. It teaches well but cannot legally be adapted, so no amount of effort can repair its reusability.

    ![Echo the Loon thinking](../../img/mascot/thinking.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Notice that quality and reusability turn independently, which is why they get separate subtotals. A resource that is easy to reuse but weak is an invitation to improve it, and that is the opportunity this book teaches you to seize.

## Recording and Reporting

A **rubric worksheet** is the form on which a reviewer records the scores and the evidence for each metric. It has one row per metric, and each row has a score, an evidence citation, and space for notes and for a second reviewer's score. Using a form keeps every review the same shape, so reviews can be compared and checked. The book's [Sample Rubric Worksheet](../../workshops/sample-rubric-worksheet.md) is a ready-to-use version with all ten metrics.

A filled-in row for Sam's chapter shows how the pieces fit. The table gives three of the ten rows.

| Metric | Score | Evidence citation | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------------------|-------|
| Accuracy and Currency | 6 | Page 14: mitochondria "only" organelle with DNA; Section 3: ribosomes "only in cells with a nucleus"; reference items 3 and 7: broken links (checked on the review date) | Two factual errors and two broken links |
| Accessibility | 3 | Figures 1 to 4: no alt text; video: no captions; headings skip level 3 (read the page source) | Most checks fail |
| Open License | 10 | Footer reads "CC BY 4.0" and links to the license text | Clear and open |

A sensible order for working through the worksheet is:

1. Fill in the review context card and complete the review checklist.
2. Read or skim the whole resource once without scoring.
3. Go metric by metric, recording the findings first and the score second.
4. Add the subtotals, the total score, and the band.
5. Exchange worksheets with a second reviewer, compare scores, and discuss any gap of more than 3 points.
6. Write the improvement list from the lowest-scoring metrics.

A **review checklist** is a short list of yes-or-no checks that a reviewer completes before scoring. It confirms that the review can start. A reviewer asks whether the whole resource is available, whether it opens and displays correctly, whether the audience and the learning outcomes are known, and whether a license statement is present. If an answer is no, the reviewer resolves it first. Scoring a resource when the audience is unknown, for example, makes every judgment about readability a guess.

A **review report** is the document that carries the result of a review to the people who decide. A useful report has six parts:

1. The resource's title, creator, source, and license
2. The reviewer's name and the date of the review
3. The ten scores, each with its evidence citation
4. The quality subtotal, the reusability subtotal, and the total score
5. The score band and the recommended action
6. A short improvement list that names the lowest-scoring metrics first

Write the report for its reader. A principal deciding whether to adopt a textbook wants the band and the top three fixes, while a colleague who will do the revising wants every evidence citation. Many teams keep one complete report and put a half-page summary on top.

## Who Reviews, and How Far to Trust the Result

A **reviewer** is the person, or in later chapters the AI agent, who scores a resource against the rubric. No single reviewer is expert in everything. Judging accuracy needs subject knowledge, judging alignment and readability needs knowledge of the learners, judging accessibility needs familiarity with the standards, and judging license and metadata needs some library skill. The table lists four roles and what each is best placed to judge.

| Reviewer role | Best placed to judge |
|---------------|----------------------|
| Subject expert | Accuracy and Currency |
| Classroom teacher | Alignment, Readability, Practice with Feedback |
| Accessibility specialist | Accessibility |
| Librarian or OER coordinator | Open License, Metadata, Portability |

In a small school, one person may fill several roles, but at least two reviewers should score any resource that matters to an adoption decision, so that mistakes and blind spots are caught. Sam might ask a biology colleague to check accuracy and a school librarian to check the license and metadata. Chapter 12 shows how an AI agent can serve as one of the reviewers, with a human checking its work.

Independence matters. A reviewer should not score their own work, and a reviewer with a stake in the outcome, such as the author of the resource or the company that sells it, should say so and ideally step aside. A second reviewer should score before seeing the first reviewer's numbers, or the second score will be anchored by the first. Finally, record each reviewer's role on the report so readers know whose expertise each score reflects.

**Reviewer bias** is a systematic tendency that pulls a reviewer's scores away from what the evidence supports. Five are common. Leniency or harshness shifts every score up or down. The halo effect lets one strong impression color all the other scores. Anchoring lets the first score influence the ones after it. Familiarity makes a reviewer trust a well-known publisher or a friend's work. And confirmation makes a reviewer look for evidence that supports a first impression. Four habits reduce bias: attach evidence to each score, score each metric separately, have a second reviewer score independently, and decide the threshold before scoring.

!!! mascot-warning "Watch for the Halo Effect"
    ![Echo the Loon warning](../../img/mascot/warning.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A beautiful design can make every other metric look better than it is. Score each metric from its own evidence, one at a time, and don't let an early good impression pick the number for you.

**Inter-rater agreement** is the degree to which two reviewers give the same or similar scores to the same resource. It tells you whether the rubric and the reviewers can be trusted. Two simple measures work well. Exact agreement is the share of metrics where the two scores are identical. Within-one agreement is the share where the scores differ by no more than 1 point. In addition, a gap of more than 3 points on any metric is a signal to stop and discuss, because the reviewers are probably looking at different evidence.

Take Sam and a colleague, who each scored the open chapter independently. The table shows their ten scores and the difference for each metric.

| Metric | Sam | Colleague | Difference |
|--------|-----|-----------|------------|
| Accuracy and Currency | 6 | 7 | 1 |
| Alignment | 5 | 5 | 0 |
| Readability | 5 | 9 | 4 |
| Accessibility | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| Practice with Feedback | 3 | 3 | 0 |
| Open License | 10 | 10 | 0 |
| Modularity | 5 | 7 | 2 |
| Metadata | 4 | 4 | 0 |
| Editable Source | 8 | 6 | 2 |
| Portability | 7 | 8 | 1 |

The two reviewers gave identical scores on 4 of 10 metrics, so exact agreement is 40 percent. They differed by 1 point or less on 7 of 10 metrics, so within-one agreement is 70 percent. The one gap of more than 3 points is Readability, where the difference is 4. That is where Sam and the colleague should sit down together, look at the same pages, and find out why one reviewer thought the text was readable and the other did not. The totals are worth a look too. Sam's total is 56. The colleague's total is 28 + 35 = 63, so Sam's chapter lands in "Major revision needed" for one reviewer and "Adopt with improvements" for the other. A difference of 7 points moved the chapter across a band boundary, which shows why agreement matters. Chapter 14 introduces a statistic called Cohen's kappa, which corrects agreement for chance.

When reviewers differ by more than 3 points, do not average their scores. Averaging hides the disagreement and produces a number that neither reviewer believes. Instead, each reviewer points to their evidence, they look at it together, and they agree on a single score or record the disagreement and the reason for it.

**Rubric reliability** is the degree to which a rubric gives consistent scores, whether from different reviewers or from the same reviewer on different days. High agreement between reviewers is evidence of reliability. Three things improve it: clear scoring descriptors, required evidence, and a little training, such as the calibration session in Chapter 11.

**Rubric validity** is the degree to which a rubric measures what it claims to measure, here the quality and reusability of a resource for learners. Reliability is not enough on its own. A rubric could give the same scores every time and still reward the wrong things, such as a pleasing design instead of accurate content. The test of validity is whether rubric scores predict something real, such as whether students learn more from a resource that scores higher. Chapters 16 and 17 show how to compare rubric scores with student results from an A/B test.

!!! mascot-encourage "Reliable Versus Valid"
    ![Echo the Loon encouraging](../../img/mascot/encouraging.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    These two words trip up almost everyone at first, so you're in good company. Hold on to this: reliable means the rubric gives the same answer again, and valid means it's measuring the right thing. A bathroom scale that is always five pounds off is reliable but not valid.

## Summary

This chapter introduced the rubric the rest of the book uses and the habits that make its scores trustworthy. The key points are:

- OER quality is how well a resource serves a particular learner and purpose, together with how easily others can reuse it.
- The quality dimension has five metrics, and the reusability dimension has five more, and the two are independent.
- A rubric lists criteria with a scale and written descriptors; the ten-metric rubric scores each metric from 0 to 10.
- A metric score follows the evidence, and evidence names a location and an observation that another reviewer could check.
- The quality subtotal and reusability subtotal each run from 0 to 50, the total from 0 to 100, and subtotals should always be read before the total.
- A score band pairs a total with an action, and an adoption threshold should be set before scoring.
- A rubric worksheet, a review checklist, and a review report keep reviews consistent.
- Reviewers have roles and biases, inter-rater agreement shows whether scores can be trusted, and a rubric needs both reliability and validity.
