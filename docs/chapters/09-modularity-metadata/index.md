## The Open License Metric

The **open license metric** is metric 6 of the ten. It asks whether a resource carries a clear, open license, with complete credit for any material it reuses. Chapter 2 taught you to read a license, so this metric applies that skill as a score. It carries the most weight in the reusability dimension, because the license decides what the other four metrics are worth. The descriptors on Chapter 3's metric card apply: a high score means an open license, preferably CC BY, stated on every component with complete credit, and a score of 0 means no license statement or all rights reserved.

**License clarity** is how plainly a license is stated, and it is the first thing the metric checks. A clear statement names the license and its version, links to the full text, and says what it covers. An unclear one leaves a reader guessing, and guessing teachers tend to play safe and not reuse. The table shows five statements and whether each is clear.

| Statement on the page | Clear? | Why |
|-----------------------|--------|-----|
| "Licensed under CC BY 4.0," linked to the license text | Yes | Names the license, version, and text |
| "Free to use" | No | Does not say what you may do beyond reading |
| "Creative Commons" | No | Does not say which license, so the conditions are unknown |
| "Some rights reserved" | No | Does not say which |
| Footer says "CC BY 4.0," repository says "all rights reserved" | No | The two statements contradict each other |

Sam's chapter has a clear statement: the footer reads "CC BY 4.0" with a link to the license text, and the repository's license file agrees. Every image in the chapter is credited, so there are no unmarked third-party items. That is why Sam scored the metric 10.

## Modularity: Pieces That Stand Alone

The **modularity metric** is metric 7 of the ten. It asks whether a resource is built from pieces that can be used separately. A teacher in another district who wants only Sam's section on the cell membrane should be able to take it without taking the other 5,000 words. The metric scores how well that works, and Sam's chapter earned a 5. The evidence is in the table.

| Where | What | How checked |
|-------|------|-------------|
| Whole chapter | One 6,000-word file with no separate parts | Opened the source |
| Sections 3 and 4 | Five references to earlier sections, such as "as we saw above" | Read both sections |
| Diagrams | Embedded as part of the page, with no separate addresses | Looked at the page source |

**Modular content** is content built from self-contained pieces, called modules, that can be used separately or combined. The simplest image is building bricks and a sculpture. A sculpture is a single object. Bricks can be rearranged into something new. This book is built that way: its chapters, its MicroSims, and its quizzes are separate pieces, so a teacher can use one without the rest. Modularity matters because it carries the Reuse and Remix permissions from Chapter 1: the right to combine pieces means little if the pieces cannot be separated.

Sam can see how to improve the open chapter by planning a split. The table shows the plan, with each module meant to cover one outcome.

| Module | Content | Outcome served |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| Organelles overview | Sections 2 and 3, first half | Identify the main organelles and their functions |
| Nucleus and ribosomes | Sections 3, second half | Identify the main organelles and their functions |
| Plant and animal cells | New, to fill the gap from Chapter 6 | Compare plant and animal cells |
| The cell membrane | Section 4 | Explain how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves |

Three signs show that a resource is modular. Each piece opens with its own context instead of "as above." The headings name the topic of each piece, so a reader can tell what it is from the heading alone. And the pieces can be reordered without the text becoming nonsense. If you can shuffle the sections and still follow each one, the resource is modular.

A **standalone component** is a piece of content that makes sense and works without the rest of its resource. It has a title, enough context to start, definitions for the terms it uses, and its own license and credit. A simple check is the lift-out test: copy the piece into a blank page and ask whether a new reader could use it. Sam tried the test on Section 4, the membrane section. It begins with "As we saw above," uses "phospholipid bilayer" without defining it, and has no title that tells a reader what it is about. The table shows what failed and the fix.

| Test question | Result for Section 4 | Fix |
|---------------|----------------------|-----|
| Does it have a clear title? | No; the heading is "Section 4" | Rename it "The Cell Membrane" |
| Does it start without outside context? | No; it opens with "As we saw above" | Rewrite the opening to introduce the topic |
| Are its terms defined? | No; "phospholipid bilayer" is not defined | Add a definition at first use |
| Does it carry its own credit? | No; credit is only on the chapter's title page | Add a credit line to the module |

**Component granularity** is the size of the pieces. A piece can be too small to be useful, such as a single sentence, or too large to be reused, such as a whole book. A useful rule of thumb is one learning objective per component: a size that teaches one thing and can be placed in a lesson. A single image, a MicroSim, a quiz, a section on one concept, and a short lesson are all reasonable sizes, and the right one depends on how the piece will be used.

**Cross-reference dependence** is how much a piece depends on other parts of its resource through phrases such as "see above," "as in Chapter 2," or "the diagram on the previous page." Each such phrase breaks when the piece is lifted out. The fix is either to restate the needed idea in the piece or to link to the other piece by address. The table shows two repairs.

| Before | After |
|--------|-------|
| As we saw in Section 2, this is why the membrane is selectively permeable. | The membrane is selectively permeable, which means it lets some substances cross more easily than others. |
| Use the diagram above to check your answer. | Use the labeled membrane diagram (linked here) to check your answer. |

The Bloom's Taxonomy Pyramid in Chapter 6 is an example: it lives at its own address, needs no login, and appears in that chapter through a single line of code. ## The Metadata Metric and Dublin Core

The **metadata metric** is metric 8 of the ten. It asks whether a resource is described well enough for others to find it and judge whether it fits. As this book's author puts it, you can't reuse what you can't find. A resource with excellent content, a good license, and sound modules will sit unused if no search can surface it. Metadata is the description that makes searching work, and a missing one is the commonest reason that good OER stay undiscovered. Sam's chapter scored 4, because its record has a title, a weak one, and an author, and nothing else.

A **metadata record** is a set of descriptive fields about a resource, such as its title, creator, subject, and license. It works like a library catalog card: a short, structured description that lets people and search tools find and judge the resource without opening it. A record can be stored in several places. It can sit in the front matter of a file, in a separate file, in the fields of a repository's form, or inside the web page in a form that search tools can read. Whichever place you use, keep the copies in agreement. A record that says one thing in the repository and another in the file creates the same problem as the contradictory license statements earlier in this chapter.

**Dublin Core** is a simple, widely used metadata standard published by the Dublin Core Metadata Initiative, which began with a 1995 workshop in Dublin, Ohio. It defines fifteen basic elements: Title, Creator, Subject, Description, Publisher, Contributor, Date, Type, Format, Identifier, Source, Language, Relation, Coverage, and Rights. Libraries, repositories, and search tools understand it, so a record that follows it can be shared between systems. Five of the fifteen map straight onto the fields this book's rubric cares most about. ## The Six Core Fields

Dublin Core has fifteen elements, and a long form discourages people from filling it in. A record that gets filled in beats a perfect one that does not, so this book's rubric focuses on six fields: title, author, subject, grade level, license, and description. They are the ones that most affect whether a resource is found and whether a teacher can tell it fits. The next paragraphs take them one at a time, with the value Sam's record has now and the value it should have.

The **title field** holds the name of the resource. A good title is specific, saying what the resource is about, and unique enough to tell it from others. Titles like "Chapter 4," "Lesson 2," or "Untitled" mean nothing outside the original book, and a search for the topic will not find them. Sam's record says "Chapter 4." The improved value is "Cell Structure and Function," which a teacher searching for a unit on cells will recognize at once. If the resource is one of several versions, say so in the title or the description.

Three title mistakes come up often: numbering without a topic, such as "Lesson 2," jargon that only the original course used, and titles so long that search results cut them off. A quick test is to search for your title. If it returns other, different resources, make yours more specific.

The **author field** names the people or organizations who created the resource. Use the creators, not the publisher or the website, and write names the same way every time so that searches match. A persistent identifier such as an ORCID, a unique number that stays with a researcher across name changes, helps where it exists. When a resource is adapted, record both roles. Sam's improved record lists Ana Rivera as the creator and Sam as the adapter, which also supports the credit requirements from Chapter 2. The table shows the two roles.

| Role | Value |
|------|-------|
| Creator | Ana Rivera |
| Adapter, contributor | Sam |

Write names the same way each time, for example family name first, so that searches match. For an organization, give its full name once. List authors in the order the creators prefer, and if the resource has many contributors, record the main creators in this field and the rest in a contributor field.

The **subject field** says what the resource is about. Give both a broad subject and a specific one, so that a teacher browsing "Biology" and a teacher searching "cell biology" both find it. Sam's record is blank. The improved value is "Biology; Cell biology." Subjects work best when drawn from an agreed list, which the section on controlled vocabulary explains.

Two or three subjects are usually enough. Avoid course codes, which mean something only inside one school, and avoid the names of products or publishers, which say where a resource came from and not what it is about.

The **grade level field** says who the resource is for. A teacher choosing a resource needs to know at a glance whether it suits their students, so this field is one of the most used. Give a grade range or an education level, using the same scheme every time, and avoid vague words like "easy." Sam's record is blank, and the improved value is "Grades 9 to 10." The audience in Chapter 6's review context card is the source: a field in the record should state what the reviewer already worked out.

Schemes for grade level differ by country and school system, so say which you use. The table shows a common U.S. scheme.

| Education level | Typical grades |
|-----------------|----------------|
| Elementary | Kindergarten to grade 5 |
| Middle school | Grades 6 to 8 |
| High school | Grades 9 to 12 |
| Undergraduate | College years 1 to 4 |
| Adult and professional | Beyond formal schooling |

The **license field** records the license in a form that people and tools can read. It should name the license and its version and link to the full text, and it should match the license statement on the page. A record that is blank, or that says only "free," forces every adopter to hunt for the license, and many will not. Sam's record is blank even though the page says "CC BY 4.0." The improved value is "CC BY 4.0, https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/."

The copyright holder and year belong near the license, for example "© 2017 Ana Rivera, CC BY 4.0." If part of the resource has a different license, such as an image from another source, say so in the record or point to the credits page, so that the field does not claim more than it should.

The **description field** is a short summary of what the resource covers and for whom. Two or three sentences are enough. A good description names the topic and the level, mentions what is included, and notes any prerequisites. It is also what a search result shows, so it is the part a teacher reads when deciding whether to click. Sam's improved description reads: "A chapter for grades 9 to 10 that introduces the main parts of plant and animal cells and explains how the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves. It includes four diagrams and three review questions." Write the description for a stranger, in plain words, and leave out praise like "amazing," which tells the reader nothing.

A good description answers five questions. What is it? Who is it for? What does it include? What does a reader need to know first? What format is it in? Sam's description answers the first three, and a fuller version would add that readers should already know what a protein is, which the chapter assumes.

!!! mascot-tip "Write the Description Last, for a Stranger"
    ![Echo the Loon giving a tip](../../img/mascot/tip.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    Imagine your description appearing as one search result among ten. Would a stranger know in two sentences what the resource covers, who it's for, and what's in it? If not, rewrite it.

Two ideas help with the subject field. **Keywords** are words or short phrases that describe a resource's topics and that a searcher might type. They are free-form: the author chooses them. Five to ten is typical, and they should include the synonyms people use. Sam's keywords are "cell, organelle, membrane, plant cell, animal cell, mitochondria."

When you choose keywords, think of the words a teacher would type into a search box, and include both the technical term and the everyday one. Repositories often let you pick from their own list first and add free-form keywords afterward. A **controlled vocabulary** is an agreed list of terms that everyone uses for the same idea, so that one concept is described one way. Without one, three resources about the same topic might be tagged "cell biology," "Cell Biology," and "cytology," and a search for one would miss the others. Examples of controlled vocabularies include the ERIC Thesaurus, which educational libraries use, and the subject lists that repositories publish. Where a repository provides a list, choosing from it makes your resource easier to find.

## Educational and Machine-Readable Metadata

Learning resources have properties that library records do not, such as the level of the learners and what the resource teaches. **LRMI**, the Learning Resource Metadata Initiative, is a specification for describing those educational properties, including the type of resource, the educational level, what it teaches, and how long it takes. It was developed to extend general metadata for teaching, and today the Dublin Core Metadata Initiative maintains it. Its properties are part of Schema.org, which the next idea explains.

**Schema.org markup** is a shared vocabulary for describing things on web pages in a way that search engines can read. The vocabulary names types of things, such as a creative work, and properties of them, such as author and license. The most common way to include it is a block of JSON-LD, a format that stores structured data in a script block so that it does not change how the page looks. Before the example, here is what the lines mean. `@context` says which vocabulary is in use. `@type` says what kind of thing is described. The other lines are properties, each a name and a value.

```html
<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": ["CreativeWork", "LearningResource"],
  "name": "Cell Structure and Function",
  "author": { "@type": "Person", "name": "Ana Rivera" },
  "license": "https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/",
  "educationalLevel": "Grades 9 to 10",
  "learningResourceType": "textbook chapter",
  "teaches": "The main parts of plant and animal cells",
  "inLanguage": "en"
}
</script>
```

This block carries the same information as Sam's improved record, in a form a search tool can read without opening the chapter.

!!! mascot-encourage "JSON-LD Looks Like Code, but You Won't Write It by Hand"
    ![Echo the Loon encouraging](../../img/mascot/encouraging.png){ class="mascot-admonition-img" }
    A block of curly braces can feel like a step too far, and that's a fair reaction. You only need to recognize it and check that it matches the page. Tools, and later in this book AI agents, can write and fill it in for you.

**Machine-readable metadata** is metadata stored in a form that software can read reliably, such as JSON-LD, XML, HTML meta tags, or the front matter at the top of a file. It matters because people are not the only readers: repositories harvest records automatically, search engines index them, and the automated checks in Chapter 13 read them. This book's own pages carry metadata in the front matter at the top of each file, for example:

```yaml
---
title: "Modularity and Metadata"
description: "Explains the open license, modularity, and metadata metrics."
---
```

The `title` and `description` there are Dublin Core's Title and Description in a form a build tool can read.

## Metadata Completeness

**Metadata completeness** is the share of the required fields that have useful values. For the six core fields, a reviewer counts how many are filled in with something that helps a searcher. A field filled with a placeholder, such as "Untitled," "N/A," or "see inside," does not count, because it fills the space and does not help. The count gives a percentage that is easy to report.

Sam's record as found has two fields filled in, and one of them is the weak "Chapter 4," so it counts as one useful field of six, about 17 percent. The author field is the only useful one. That is consistent with a score of 4. After the improvements in the table above, all six fields have useful values, for a completeness of 6 of 6, or 100 percent. ## Summary

This chapter covered three reusability metrics and the evidence that supports each. The key points are:

- The open license metric checks that a clear, open license is stated, and license clarity depends on naming the license, its version, and its text.
- The modularity metric asks whether a resource comes in pieces that can be used separately, and modular content is built from standalone components of a sensible size.
- A standalone component has a title, context, defined terms, and its own credit, and cross-reference dependence breaks that when a piece is lifted out.
- An embeddable component has its own address, needs no login, allows embedding, shows its credit, and fits narrow widths.
- The metadata metric asks whether a resource is described well enough to be found, and a metadata record works like a library catalog card.
- Dublin Core defines fifteen basic elements, and this book's rubric focuses on six: title, author, subject, grade level, license, and description.
- Keywords and a controlled vocabulary make the subject field consistent, and LRMI and Schema.org markup describe educational properties in machine-readable form.
- Metadata completeness counts the fields that hold useful values.
