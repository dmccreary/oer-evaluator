# References: Foundations of Open Educational Resources

1. [Open educational resources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_educational_resources) - Wikipedia - Defines OER, traces the movement's history, and lists the 5R permissions with examples of repositories and national policies. Anchors this chapter's definition of an OER and its distinction between open and merely free materials.

2. [Copyright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copyright) - Wikipedia - Explains how copyright arises automatically, how long it lasts, and which exclusive rights it gives creators. Supplies the default rule that makes an explicit open license necessary before a teacher may copy, change, or share a resource.

3. [Free content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_content) - Wikipedia - Surveys definitions of free and open content, separating works that merely cost nothing from works that grant freedoms to reuse and modify. Supports the chapter's section explaining why free does not mean open.

4. Free Culture: How Big Media Uses Technology and the Law to Lock Down Culture and Control Creativity - Lawrence Lessig - Penguin Press (2004) - Lessig's contrast between a "free culture" and a "permission culture" shows why restrictive copyright is the default and why openly licensed work matters. His story-driven framing helps teachers see what the 5R permissions release.

5. Open: The Philosophy and Practices that are Revolutionizing Education and Science (2017) - Rajiv Jhangiani and Robert Biswas-Diener (eds.) - Ubiquity Press - Includes Robin DeRosa and Scott Robison's chapter "From OER to Open Pedagogy," which argues that students should help build open resources, not only consume them. That idea underlies this chapter's short section on open pedagogy.

6. [Definition of Open](https://opencontent.org/definition/) - opencontent.org - David Wiley's own statement of the 5R permissions (retain, reuse, revise, remix, redistribute) and why a license must grant all five. This is the source of the 5R framework used throughout the book.

7. [Open Educational Resources](https://www.unesco.org/en/open-educational-resources) - UNESCO - UNESCO's OER hub, including the 2019 Recommendation on OER, the first international standard-setting instrument for openly licensed materials. Shows how governments define OER and why they encourage adoption.

8. [OER Resources for Educators](https://creativecommons.org/about/education/education-oer-resources) - Creative Commons - A teacher's guide to finding openly licensed images, video, audio, textbooks, simulations, and courses, with pointers to OpenStax, PhET, and OER Commons. Supports the chapter's section on finding OER.

9. [A Guide to Making Open Textbooks with Students](https://press.rebus.community/makingopentextbookswithstudents/) - Rebus Community - An open guide in which instructors describe having students create open textbooks and renewable assignments. A practical follow-up to the chapter's discussion of open pedagogy and of creating OER.

10. [OpenStax](https://openstax.org/) - Rice University - A nonprofit publisher of free, peer-reviewed college and high-school textbooks under open licenses. A well-known example of an adoptable OER collection, useful for practicing the adopt-adapt-create decision.
