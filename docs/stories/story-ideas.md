# OER Story Ideas

A list of fun and engaging mini-graphic novel stories that will help you remember the key strategies in efficiently evaluating OER resources both with and without AI agents.

## How This List Was Built

The story generator was first written to inspire young readers with profiles of scientists. This book's readers are different: OER evangelists, librarians, instructional designers, and the professors and teachers who choose textbooks for a campus, a department, or a district. They have sat through the committee meeting, defended the textbook budget, and been burned by a "free" PDF. So these ideas are **fictional comedies set in places they know**: the faculty lounge, the department meeting, the library reference desk, the school board meeting, and the potluck.

Each idea is built on one thing a reader should still remember a month later, and each is tied to the chapters that teach it.

- **Humor comes from the situation, never from the learner.** The jokes land on "All rights reserved" footers, the 14th edition, `final_FINAL_v7.docx`, and committees that score everything an 8. Students, and people who use assistive technology, are always the competent ones in the room.
- **The AI agent is a character, not a villain.** Quill is an eager intern who is fast, confident, and sometimes wrong. The lesson is never "AI bad" or "AI good". It is "check the evidence".
- **Stories with and without AI are both here.** Part 1 teaches the skills a reviewer needs even with no AI at all. Part 2 adds the agent. Several stories (22, 26, 27, and 29) put the two side by side.
- **Settings cover high school, college, university, and district.** The tag in each entry says which.
- **There are no historical profiles.** Every character is invented. Campuses are described by type. The Minnesota stories may use the real names of colleges if the participants agree.

## The Recurring Cast

A small repeating cast makes the stories feel like one universe and lets readers meet the same people again in a different chapter. None is a real person.

| Character | Who they are | What they are like |
|-----------|--------------|--------------------|
| **Margie Halvorsen** | Campus librarian and OER evangelist | Wears a laminated rubric card on a lanyard. Calm. Her catchphrase is "Show me where it says that." |
| **Dr. Priya Raman** | Chemistry professor | Has used the 14th edition of the same textbook since 2009. A real skeptic, and usually right. |
| **Tomás Reyes** | Instructional designer | Accessibility zealot. Listens to his email at three times normal speed. |
| **Bao Vang** | Community college math instructor | Builds MicroSims at midnight and forgets to label them. |
| **Dean Pemberton** | Dean who loves a savings number | Wants a banner, a press release, and a ribbon-cutting. Means well. |
| **Quill** | The AI agent | An eager intern with a clipboard who never says "I don't know". Uses *they/them*. Drawn the same way in every story. |
| **Echo** | Echo the Loon, the book's mascot | A background cameo in every story, with a small magnifying glass: a **Where's Echo?** game for readers. In the winter Minnesota story Echo has migrated south and appears only on a postcard. |

Students appear by first name (Kiara, Marcus, Noor) and are always sharper than the adults expect.

## Art Direction

The standard skill picks an art style by historical era. These stories are contemporary, so use one consistent house style and let each story borrow a genre for fun.

- **House style:** flat cartoon, modern vector, clean lines, matching Echo's pose set. Palette of indigo (`#3949ab`) and burnt orange (`#b45309`) with warm neutrals.
- **Genre costumes:** a few stories borrow a look for the joke, such as film noir (7), a game show (2), a home-makeover show (21), a storybook fable (22), a county fair (5), and a forensic procedural (17). Name the genre in the cover prompt.
- **No brand names, logos, or real interface screenshots** in any image prompt. Draw generic stand-ins. No real people.
- **Numbers are illustrative** unless the entry says it comes from a chapter. A story that shows a score should say "illustrative" in its Narrative Prompt and keep the numbers consistent with the rubric in Chapter 3.

## Part 1: Evaluating Without AI

These stories teach what a reviewer needs even if no AI agent is ever opened. An agent can only check what a person knows to ask.

### 1. The Free Textbook That Wasn't

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Community college faculty lounge, the Tuesday before fall semester (college) |
| **Theme** | Free is not the same as open |
| **Connection** | Chapter 1: OER definition, the 5R permissions, free versus open |
| **Panels** | **6**: one technique, one gag per permission |
| **Look** | House style, with a red rubber stamp that recurs |

Dr. Raman announces that she has adopted a free chemistry PDF and saved her students $289. Then she tries to do the five things OER allows, and each attempt earns a red "NOPE" stamp: keep a copy, reuse a chapter in class, fix a typo, add a table from another source, and hand the file to a colleague. Margie slides a CC BY chemistry text across the table, and each of the five stamps turns green.

*What readers will remember:* Price and permission are two separate dials. Open means you may retain, reuse, revise, remix, and redistribute.

### 2. Adopt, Adapt, or Create: The Game Show

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University "Textbook Affordability Week" assembly (university) |
| **Theme** | Match the effort to the need |
| **Connection** | Chapter 1: adopt, adapt, create; Chapter 18: building a component library. See the Adopt, Adapt, or Create Pathway Chooser MicroSim |
| **Panels** | **7**: setup, three doors, aftermath |
| **Look** | Game show, with studio lights and a laugh-track banner |

Dean Pemberton hosts a game show in which three faculty contestants each pick a door. Door One is **Adopt**: use it as is, and a sparkling stack of existing books waits behind it. Door Two is **Adapt**: revise or remix, which means a pleasant afternoon with an editable file. Door Three is **Create**, and behind it sits one exhausted professor and a calendar showing 800 hours. Margie, as the "phone-a-friend", runs the rubric for each contestant and steers two of them to the cheaper doors. A student in the front row holds up a $289 receipt as the studio audience applauds.

*What readers will remember:* Look for an existing resource first, change it second, and build from nothing last.

### 3. The Potluck Label Law

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | High school staff room, Teacher Appreciation Potluck (high school) |
| **Theme** | Credit where it is due |
| **Connection** | Chapter 2: attribution, the TASL format (Title, Author, Source, License), noting a change |
| **Panels** | **6**: one technique, before and after |
| **Look** | House style, with every dish shown with a small index card |

A new principal decrees that every dish needs a TASL card. The first cards are a disaster: "Recipe from the internet." "Grandma's." Then the real test arrives, a wild rice hotdish adapted from a 1974 church cookbook with the soup swapped out. The correct card names the title, the author, the source, and the license ("share it, but return the pan"), and adds a line saying what was changed. The principal's own brownies turn out to be a remix too.

*What readers will remember:* Title, Author, Source, License, and a note when you changed something.

### 4. License Speed-Dating

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Open Education Week mixer in a campus gym (college or university) |
| **Theme** | Not every license can combine with every other |
| **Connection** | Chapter 2: the Creative Commons elements (BY, SA, NC, ND), license compatibility, the NonCommercial gray area. See the License Compatibility Checker and NonCommercial Gray Area Sorter MicroSims |
| **Panels** | **8**: five matches, one mismatch, one gray-area interlude, one resolution |
| **Look** | Speed-dating mixer with name tags, a bell, and a snack table |

Six licenses wear name tags and take turns at small tables. **BY** just wants credit. **BY-SA** will share, but only with those who share alike. **BY-ND** says "don't change a thing." **BY-NC** wants nothing commercial. Two matches go well. The BY-SA and BY-NC match ends politely and permanently: irreconcilable differences. A chaperone by the snack table asks whether the PTA selling brownies to fund a course pack counts as commercial, and the whole room goes quiet. Margie closes with a seating chart of what combines with what.

*What readers will remember:* ND blocks remixes, SA passes its terms on, NC has a gray area, and some pairs simply cannot combine.

!!! note "Check before drafting"
    The compatibility pairs must match the rules in Chapter 2 exactly. Check them there before writing the dialogue.

### 5. Blue Ribbon Casserole

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | County fair hotdish contest, then Monday on campus (college) |
| **Theme** | Quality and reusability are different questions |
| **Connection** | Chapter 3: five quality and five reusability metrics, reporting two subtotals, not one blend. See the Quality and Reusability Quadrant Sorter MicroSim |
| **Panels** | **8**: three contest entries, the verdict, and the Monday echo |
| **Look** | County fair, with ribbons and a judging table |

Margie volunteers as a judge at the county fair hotdish contest, and each entry teaches a quadrant. Entry A is the best thing anyone has tasted, but the recipe is "a pinch of this" on a napkin, and the cook will not share it: high quality, low reusability. Entry B has a flawless recipe card with photos and a printable version, and it tastes like wet cardboard: low quality, high reusability. Entry C is delicious and shareable, and takes the ribbon. On Monday Margie opens two chemistry textbooks and sees Entry A and Entry B all over again.

*What readers will remember:* Report quality and reusability separately. A great resource nobody can reuse and a perfectly portable bad one both need work.

### 6. final_FINAL_v7_reallyfinal

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University department shared drive (university) |
| **Theme** | Open, editable source makes improvement possible |
| **Connection** | Chapter 4: open file formats, Markdown, editable source, version control, fork, pull request |
| **Panels** | **7**: a linear fix, from chaos to a clean history |
| **Look** | House style, with a hallway of identical folder icons |

The department wants to fix one chapter of a shared syllabus and a PDF-only textbook. The drive holds `SyllabusFinal`, `SyllabusFINAL2`, and `SyllabusFINAL_USETHIS`, and a Track Changes document is a rainbow of seven colors. Dr. Raman refuses to touch anything with the word "code" in it. Margie shows that the open version is a plain text file with some pound signs, and that version control is a time machine with a memory. A "fork" is your own copy, and a "pull request" is politely handing someone your edit with a note. Priya makes her first pull request, with the commit message "fix typo", and then makes it forty more times.

*What readers will remember:* An editable source plus a history is what lets strangers fix your book.

### 7. The Case of the Dead Link

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University library, 4:55 p.m. on the Friday before finals (university) |
| **Theme** | Accuracy and currency decay, and good citations resist it |
| **Connection** | Chapter 5: fact checking, citation quality, link rot, outdated content, persistent identifiers, archived copies |
| **Panels** | **9**: mystery, three clues, a reveal, and a multi-step fix |
| **Look** | Film noir, black and white with a single orange accent |

A world-weary narrator (Margie in a trench coat) takes the case of a textbook that cites a web page that has vanished. The victim is a 2011 URL. The clues: a "newest" social network that no longer exists, a sentence about a phone with a physical keyboard, and three other links that return 404. The witnesses: a persistent identifier that still resolves, an archived snapshot, and a newer source that says the number changed. The case closes with a list of what to fix and a note saying why the DOI survived and the plain URL did not.

*What readers will remember:* Links rot, so cite stable sources, save an archived copy, and check the date.

### 8. Grade 14 in Room 9

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | High school ninth-grade biology class (high school) |
| **Theme** | Readability is about fit, not dumbing down |
| **Connection** | Chapter 6: reading level, Flesch-Kincaid grade level, plain language, fit to audience |
| **Panels** | **6**: one problem, one fix |
| **Look** | House style, with a sentence stretched down a hallway like police tape |

Noor, the student newspaper editor, runs the opening paragraph of the ninth-grade biology textbook through a readability checker. The result: grade 14.2. One sentence runs 74 words and has to be diagrammed across a whole hallway. The author insists this is "rigor". Noor and Margie rewrite the paragraph in plain language, keep every technical term, define each one on first use, and drop the reading level to grade 9. Nobody's science got easier. It just got readable.

*What readers will remember:* A textbook can have hard ideas and easy sentences. Match the reading level to the reader.

### 9. What the Screen Reader Said

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | College instructional design office (college) |
| **Theme** | Accessibility is something you can hear when it fails |
| **Connection** | Chapter 7: alt text, headings, WCAG 2.1 AA, screen readers, automated accessibility checkers |
| **Panels** | **7**: a demonstration, a pattern, and a fix |
| **Look** | House style, with sound waves drawn as speech balloons |

Kiara, a student who uses a screen reader every day, offers to read the professor's new chapter aloud. The room hears: "Image. Figure underscore three underscore final underscore v2 dot jpg. Graphic. Graphic. Graphic." Next come the headings, which are bold text and not real headings, so the reader announces "no headings found" and navigation is a ten-minute scroll. Tomás hands over a new rule: alt text answers "what does this image do here?" and not "what does it look like?" The same river photo gets alt text mentioning the bends in one chapter and no alt text in another where it is decoration. Kiara reads the fixed page back at triple speed and grades it.

*What readers will remember:* Alt text says what the image does here, and real headings make a page navigable.

### 10. The Fluorescent Yellow Textbook

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | College dorm study room (college) |
| **Theme** | Re-reading feels like learning and often is not |
| **Connection** | Chapter 8: worked examples, formative assessment, retrieval practice, question quality, immediate feedback |
| **Panels** | **6**: before, exam, after |
| **Look** | House style, with the page glowing yellow |

Marcus highlights every line of his textbook until the page glows like a traffic cone, then fails the exam. His study partner has a different textbook: a worked example first, then a question, then feedback that explains *why* an answer is wrong. A second textbook, in a gag panel, has a quiz where every answer is C and the feedback is the single word "Incorrect." The partner explains retrieval practice: close the book and try to say it. Marcus's highlighter, now dry, becomes a bookmark.

*What readers will remember:* Practice with feedback beats highlighting. Feedback should teach, not just grade.

### 11. The Orphan MicroSim

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University library, reference desk (university) |
| **Theme** | You can't reuse what you can't find |
| **Connection** | Chapter 9: metadata, Dublin Core, the six core fields, metadata completeness |
| **Panels** | **7**: a loss, a search, and a rescue |
| **Look** | House style, with a lost-and-found bin of sad little objects |

Bao built a superb interactive slope simulation in 2022. It lives in a folder named `untitled-3` with no description, no subject, no grade level, and no license. A colleague in another state needs exactly that simulation and searches for "interactive slope" for an hour. In the lost-and-found at the library desk, the simulation wears a name tag reading "Hello, my name is: Untitled." Margie fills in six fields of metadata, and the same search now finds it in four seconds. A final panel shows the colleague's course page with the simulation embedded and Bao credited.

*What readers will remember:* Good metadata is what makes a resource findable, and findable comes before reusable.

### 12. The Chapter That Wouldn't Lift Out

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Community college math department (college) |
| **Theme** | A good component stands alone |
| **Connection** | Chapter 9: modularity; Chapter 10: portability, no-login access, iframe embedding, LMS import, platform lock-in |
| **Panels** | **8**: the lift attempt, three snags, the redesign, and a clean lift |
| **Look** | Knitting and craft, with a sweater whose threads stand in for links |

Bao pulls one section out of a textbook to drop into her course, and it will not come loose, like a thread in a sweater. The section says "as we saw in Chapter 3" fourteen times. Its images are hotlinked and vanish. The interactive graph needs a login to a platform that has since been acquired and shut down. After the lift test fails three times, the section is redesigned with its own objective, stated prerequisites, local images, and a no-login embed. The last panel is the same section pasted into a course page in one try.

*What readers will remember:* Apply the lift-out test: can it stand alone, open without an account, and move without breaking?

### 13. The Floor Beneath the Total

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | College department meeting, 4 p.m. on a Thursday (college) |
| **Theme** | A high total can hide a failed metric |
| **Connection** | Chapter 3: bands and the adoption threshold; Chapter 11: adopt, revise, or reject, boundary cases |
| **Panels** | **8**: the argument, the rule, three boundary cases, and a fix |
| **Look** | Game show, with a limbo bar set at 5 |

A committee reviews a textbook that scores **88**. Dean Pemberton wants to adopt it today. Tomás points out that accessibility scored **4**, and a student who uses a screen reader enrolls on Monday. Margie lays out the rule on a flip chart: Adopt needs a total of at least 75 **and** every metric at 5 or above, which she illustrates with a limbo bar set at 5. The Dean pleads "But it's 88!", and Margie walks him through the boundary cases: 74 is Revise, 75 with every metric at 5 or above is Adopt, 50 is Revise, and 49 is Reject. The cheap fix, alt text and captions, is done by Friday, and the re-score clears the floor.

*What readers will remember:* Adopt means 75 or more with nothing below 5. Never report a total without the lowest metric.

## Part 2: Evaluating With AI Agents

These stories introduce Quill and the habits that make an agent's report trustworthy. None of them is anti-AI. Each is about checking the evidence.

### 14. Quill, the Eager Intern

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University OER office (university) |
| **Theme** | The agent makes the first pass and the human makes the call |
| **Connection** | Chapter 12: AI assistants, agents, skills, rubric scoring, evidence for a score; Chapter 13: human in the loop |
| **Panels** | **8**: an arrival, a miracle, a catch, and a working routine |
| **Look** | House style, with Quill drawn as an eager intern holding a clipboard |

Margie hands a 400-page textbook to Quill, and four minutes later there is a ten-metric report with scores and a confident tone. Dr. Raman's eyebrow rises. Margie says her catchphrase and asks for the evidence: every score has to quote the text it came from. Most quotes are exact. One is "paraphrased", and one points at a page that is not in the book. By the last panel the three-step routine is on the wall: the agent proposes, the human checks the evidence, and the human decides.

*What readers will remember:* The agent is fast, the evidence is what you trust, and you make the decision.

### 15. Weak Prompt, Strong Prompt

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Faculty technology workshop (college or university) |
| **Theme** | A good prompt works like a good order |
| **Connection** | Chapter 12: prompts, rubric prompts, structured output |
| **Panels** | **6**: a side-by-side comparison |
| **Look** | House style, split down the middle, with two coffee orders |

Two professors sit side by side. One types "Is this textbook good?" and Quill writes a warm essay ending with "a delightful journey!" and no numbers. The other writes a prompt like an order for a very specific coffee: the role, the rubric, the audience, the rule that each score must quote evidence, the output format, and the line "anything untested counts as failed". The first gets a poem, and the second gets a report she can check. The final panel is the two orders on a coffee shop board.

*What readers will remember:* Rubric, evidence rule, format, and "untested counts as failed" are what a strong prompt contains.

### 16. The Privacy Officer Has Entered the Chat

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | High school teachers' room (high school) |
| **Theme** | Review the resource, not the students |
| **Connection** | Chapter 13: student data privacy, FERPA, AI cost, human in the loop |
| **Panels** | **6**: a mistake, an apparition, and a rule |
| **Look** | House style, with a gentle ghost gag |

A well-meaning teacher pastes the whole grade roster, with names and support notes, into a chatbot to "personalize feedback". A translucent district privacy officer rises from the chat window holding a clipboard. Nobody is shamed. The officer explains the rule in one line: an agent that reviews a *resource* needs only the resource. The teacher also gets a look at the usage meter, which ticks like a taxi. The last panel is a three-item checklist on the staff-room wall that ends with "when unsure, ask your privacy officer".

*What readers will remember:* Keep student information out of every prompt. Ask before you send. Mind the meter.

!!! note "Keep it non-legal"
    The chapter says this book does not give legal advice. The story should end with a pointer to the school's own privacy officer, and never with a legal conclusion.

### 17. The Citation That Never Was

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University library reference desk (university) |
| **Theme** | If you cannot find it, treat it as false |
| **Connection** | Chapter 14: hallucination and its four forms (feature, quotation, measurement, source), invented citation; Chapter 5: citation quality |
| **Panels** | **9**: a mystery, a reveal, and a multi-step fix |
| **Look** | Forensic procedural, with evidence markers numbered like a crime scene |

Quill's report praises a textbook as well sourced and cites a journal article by two authors, with volume, issue, and page numbers. It reads perfectly. Margie checks the catalog, the DOI lookup, the publisher's table of contents, and an interlibrary loan request. The journal exists, but volume 14 never had that issue. Quill's defense: "It sounded right." Margie sets out the four ways an agent invents things, adds a column to the report called "found by a human", and tells the class the one-line rule.

*What readers will remember:* A fluent, confident citation is not evidence. Look it up.

### 18. The License Quill Misread

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Community college OER office (college) |
| **Theme** | A license is what it says, not what it sounds like |
| **Connection** | Chapter 14: license misreading; Chapter 2: reading a license statement. See the Anatomy of a License Statement MicroSim |
| **Panels** | **6**: two misreads, one catch |
| **Look** | House style, with a footer shown in close-up |

The footer of a textbook reads "© 2019 Creative Commons Learning Network. All rights reserved." Quill sees the words "Creative Commons", declares the book CC0 and public domain, and scores the license metric 10. Dean Pemberton is about to approve a remix. Margie reads the footer aloud. Then Quill reads a CC BY-NC-ND license as "free to remix", and Margie reaches for the license deed. The story ends with a stamp: "Read the license, not the logo."

*What readers will remember:* Agents pattern-match on names. Verify every license against its actual terms.

### 19. The Pizza Menu Scores 82

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University OER office (university) |
| **Theme** | Calibrate the agent with a decoy |
| **Connection** | Chapter 14: overconfident score, score inflation, calibration against human scores; Chapter 3: score bands |
| **Panels** | **7**: a setup, a surprise, a recompute, and a fix |
| **Look** | House style, with a pizza menu treated like a serious academic text |

Before trusting Quill with real textbooks, Margie slips in a decoy: a pizza restaurant menu. It has no learning outcomes, one source (a phone number), and a typo in "pepperoni". Quill returns **82 out of 100**, praising it as "engaging, accessible, and highly practical". Margie recomputes one metric from Quill's own checks: 6 of 10 passed, which earns a 5 or 6 (Adequate), but Quill reported an 8. The menu's stated learning outcome is "hunger". She adds the rule that untested counts as failed and demands a tool log, and the pizza menu drops to a sensible **31** (illustrative).

*What readers will remember:* Test an agent with a resource you already know is bad. A high score with thin evidence is overconfident.

### 20. The Standards Code That Never Was

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | High school district curriculum office and school board meeting (high school, district) |
| **Theme** | Alignment is checked against the real standards document |
| **Connection** | Chapter 6: learning outcomes, standards alignment, Bloom's levels; Chapter 14: hallucination |
| **Panels** | **7**: a request, a table, a public question, and a fix |
| **Look** | House style, with a board room in the evening |

The district curriculum coordinator asks Quill to map a biology unit to the state standards, and gets a gorgeous table. At the board meeting a parent asks what one of the codes in the table actually says. Nobody knows, because the code matches nothing in the state's document. A student teacher finds the actual standards document, and the team re-maps the unit against the real text. Along the way they notice the unit's outcomes say "understand", which no one can measure, and swap in verbs a quiz can check.

*What readers will remember:* Never accept alignment from memory. Check the code against the source, and write outcomes you can measure.

### 21. The Great Fix-It Weekend

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | Community college math department, a Saturday and Sunday (college) |
| **Theme** | Improve one metric at a time, then review every change |
| **Connection** | Chapter 15: improvement plan, the Fix Toolkit, controlling the changes, derivative work, re-scoring |
| **Panels** | **10**: a before, six fixes in a montage, one near-disaster, and an after |
| **Look** | Home-makeover show, with reveal curtains and a before-and-after score |

Bao's 2014 algebra notes PDF scores a modest **52** (illustrative), with no alt text, fake headings, a high reading level, no metadata, and no practice. Over a weekend, Quill and Bao fix them in order of impact, in a makeover montage. In the middle of the montage Quill "improves" an equation and flips a sign. Bao catches it because she reviews every change. Margie checks that the license allows a revised copy and that the credit stays attached. On Sunday evening they re-score, and the reveal curtains open on **78** (illustrative).

*What readers will remember:* Fix the lowest metric first, re-score, review every change, and keep the credit.

### 22. The Tortoise, the Hare, and the Rubric

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | University departments with one deadline (university) |

## Part 3: Proving the Change Helped

### 24. Two Sections, One Sneaky Schedule

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | High school biology department (high school) |
| **Theme** | A test can fool you if you design it carelessly |
| **Connection** | Chapter 16: control and treatment groups, random assignment, confounding variables, sampling bias, pre-test and post-test, learning gain, informed consent |
| **Panels** | **8**: a plan, three traps, a redesign, and a clean test |
| **Look** | House style, with a pep rally as the recurring villain |

A teacher wants to test an improved *Watershed Basics*. The first plan: first period uses the old version and seventh period uses the new one. Margie points out that seventh period comes after lunch and a pep rally, which is a confounding variable. The second plan: let volunteers use the new version. Now the eager students are all in one group, and that is sampling bias. The third plan sends the consent letters after the pre-test, which is backward. The final design uses blocked random assignment within each class, consent first, the same pre-test and post-test for both groups, and an equally good activity for students who decline.

*What readers will remember:* Randomize, get consent first, and watch for anything else that differs between the two groups.

### 25. Large Effect, Small Sample

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | College dean's office (college) |
| **Theme** | A big number from a small test deserves a second look |
| **Connection** | Chapter 17: comparing group means, spread, effect size, small-sample caution, checking rubric scores against outcomes |
| **Panels** | **7**: a triumph, a hush, and an honest report |
| **Look** | House style, with a banner under construction |

The *Watershed Basics* pilot has 12 students per group, and the effect size comes back at 1.03. Dean Pemberton orders a banner reading "OER IMPROVEMENT: LARGE EFFECT!" ### 26. Minnesota Nice Is Not a Scoring Method

| | |
|---|---|
| **Setting** | A statewide video call of five reviewers from five campuses (college and university) |
| **Theme** | Politeness inflates scores |
| **Connection** | Chapter 3: reliability, evidence for a score; Chapter 11: calibration and consensus; Chapter 14: score inflation. See the Reviewer Bias Spotter and Inter-Rater Agreement Explorer MicroSims |
| **Panels** | **7**: a polite review, a translation, a standoff, and a rule |
| **Look** | House style, with subtitles that translate what people really mean |

Five exceedingly polite reviewers score the same sample textbook. Every score lands between 7 and 9. The comments are "That's... different." "It has a lot of... content." "I'm sure the author tried." Subtitles translate: "interesting" means 3, and "has potential" means 4. Margie points out that this is leniency bias and that the group is inflating scores exactly the way an overconfident agent does. The fix is the book's own: score alone first, quote the evidence, then discuss only gaps larger than 3 points. For the first time, someone says, "I... respectfully... disagree." Then there is the Midwest goodbye, which takes eleven minutes.

*What readers will remember:* Humans inflate scores too. Calibration needs evidence, blind scoring first, and permission to disagree.

## How to Generate a Story

To turn any idea into a full graphic novel with generated images, use:

> book-media-generator (story route): {Story Title} --panels {N}

The suggested panel count is in each entry, and `--panels N` overrides it. At about $0.039 per image the cost is $0.039 × (N + 1). A 6-panel story costs about $0.27 and a 12-panel story about $0.51. All 29 stories together are 252 images, about **$9.83**.

Before generating any story:

1. Read `CONTENT-GENERATION-GUIDE.md`. Student-facing prose follows the book's writing rules.
2. Keep the cast consistent with the table above, and put Echo in the background of at least one panel.
3. Keep brand names and logos out of every image prompt.
4. Generate the cover first and check it before spending credits on the panels.
5. Add the story to the Stories section of `mkdocs.yml`, add a card to `docs/stories/index.md`, and run `mkdocs build --strict`.
