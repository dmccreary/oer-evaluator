    OER Evaluator Instructor's Guide¶

    Welcome to the instructor's guide for OER Evaluator: Evaluating and Improving Open Educational Resources with AI Agents. This guide explains every feature of the textbook, how to use it in a course or workshop, and how to make your own customized copy. No technical background is assumed. Every technical term is defined before it is used.

    About This Intelligent Textbook¶

    What Is an Intelligent Textbook?¶

    An intelligent textbook is a digital textbook that goes beyond static text and images. It adds interactive simulations, self-check quizzes, a searchable glossary, and a structured map of how concepts depend on each other. The goal is a richer experience than a printed book can give.

    The Five Levels of Intelligent Textbooks¶

    Intelligent textbooks fall into five levels, based on how interactive and adaptive they are:

    Level Name Description Example Features
    Level 1 Static Digital A PDF or basic web version of a print textbook Text and images only
    Level 2 Interactive Adds simulations, quizzes, and searchable glossaries MicroSims, self-check quizzes, concept search
    Level 3 Adaptive Adjusts content to student performance Personalized learning paths
    Level 4 AI-Assisted Includes an AI tutor that answers student questions Chatbot integration, automated feedback
    Level 5 Fully Adaptive AI Learns from student interactions and optimizes itself Real-time content generation

    This textbook is a Level 2 Interactive Intelligent Textbook. It has 51 MicroSims, a quiz for every chapter, a glossary, a FAQ, and a learning graph. It does not track individual students or adapt to them.

    What Makes This Textbook Different¶

    • A repeatable method. The book teaches one 10-metric rubric (five quality metrics and five reusability metrics, 10 points each) and applies it by hand, then with AI agents.
    • Evidence over opinion. Every chapter asks learners to name the evidence behind a score. The skill matters most when checking an AI agent's confident answer.
    • Improvement, not just judgment. Later chapters use AI agents to raise a resource's score, then test whether the change helped with a manual A/B test with students.
    • Interactive MicroSims. Learners manipulate small models in the browser with no software to install.
    • A learning graph. A visual map of all 351 concepts and their prerequisites.
    • A mascot. Echo the Loon, a friendly guide who appears in the student chapters.
    • Free and open. The book is licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so you may copy and adapt it for non-commercial teaching.

    Who Should Use This Book¶

    The target audience is teachers (K-12 and college), instructional designers, librarians, and OER coordinators who select, adapt, or create open educational resources. There are no prerequisites. Readers should be comfortable with a web browser and a word processor. Experience with an AI chat assistant helps but is not required, because the book teaches the AI-agent skills it uses.

    The Course Description lists the learning outcomes, organized by the six levels of the 2001 Bloom taxonomy, and the topics the book deliberately does not cover.

    Using the Chapters¶

    Chapter Structure¶

    The book has 19 chapters in a deliberate sequence. Each chapter builds on earlier ones, so learners should work through them in order.

    Chapters Topic Area
    1 Foundations: what OER are, the 5R permissions, adopt/adapt/create
    2 Open licenses and attribution
    3 The 10-metric rubric and scoring
    4 Open file formats and version control
    5-8 Quality metrics: accuracy and currency, alignment and readability, accessibility, practice and feedback
    9-10 Reusability metrics: modularity and metadata, portability and reuse
    11 Manual evaluation and adoption decisions
    12-14 AI agents: prompts and rubric scoring, automated checks, cost and privacy, checking and calibrating agent output
    15 Improving a resource with AI agents
    16-17 Designing and analyzing an A/B test with students
    18 Reusable components and publishing OER
    19 Training teachers and certifying reviewers

    The List of Chapters shows every chapter with its summary.

    What Each Chapter Contains¶

    1. Front matter. Metadata at the top of the file (title, description, version). Students do not see it. Search engines and the site builder do.
    2. Summary. A short overview of what the chapter covers.
    3. Concepts covered. The specific concepts from the learning graph that the chapter addresses.
    4. Prerequisites. Links to earlier chapters.
    5. Main content. The core material, with tables, worked examples, and embedded MicroSims.
    6. Mascot admonitions. Short callout boxes from Echo (see Meet Echo below).
    7. Key takeaways. A numbered summary of the most important ideas.
    8. Quiz and references. Separate pages linked under each chapter in the left navigation.

    Suggested Classroom Use¶

    • Before class. Assign the chapter as reading. The MicroSims keep independent reading active.
    • During class. Project a MicroSim and ask learners to predict what happens when you move a slider, then test the prediction.
    • After class. Assign the chapter quiz as a self-check, then have learners apply the rubric to a real resource.
    • Pacing. Plan roughly two to three class periods (90 to 135 minutes) per chapter. Chapters with more MicroSims take longer. For a short course, see the formats below.

    Course Formats¶

    Format Suggested Path
    One-day workshop Chapters 1-3, 11, 15 and a hands-on scoring practice. See the workshop materials and the Sample One-Day Schedule.
    Manual-evaluation unit Chapters 1-11 with the Sample Rubric Worksheet
    AI-agent unit Chapters 12-15 after learners can score by hand
    Research or capstone project Chapters 16-17, designing and analyzing an A/B test
    Reviewer training Chapter 19, built on the earlier chapters

    Teach the manual rubric first

    Learners who score by hand before using an AI agent can tell when the agent is wrong. Chapter 14 depends on that skill.

    Using the MicroSims¶

    What Is a MicroSim?¶

    A MicroSim (micro-simulation) is a small interactive model that runs in a web browser. Learners change one or more variables with sliders, buttons, or drag-and-drop and see the result immediately. No software installation is needed. MicroSims work in any modern browser (Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge) on a computer, tablet, or phone.

    How MicroSims Are Embedded¶

    MicroSims appear in the chapter text as rectangular interactive areas. They are embedded with an iframe, a web feature that shows one web page inside another. You do not need to understand iframes. The MicroSim loads automatically when a learner opens the page.

    The book has 51 MicroSims. Browse them all on the MicroSims list. A few examples:

    MicroSim What It Teaches
    Creative Commons License Switches How each license element changes what you may do
    Total Score Calculator How ten metric scores combine into one 100-point total
    Weak and Strong Prompt Why specific prompts produce better agent scoring
    Inter-Rater Agreement Explorer How to measure whether two reviewers agree
    Five Confounders What can spoil an A/B test

    Tips for Using MicroSims in Class¶

    1. Project them. Ask learners to call out a prediction before you change a control.
    2. Let learners explore. After a demonstration, give them five to ten minutes to experiment on their own devices.
    3. Reset and retry. Encourage learners to reset a MicroSim and try a different scenario.
    4. Return to the text. Each MicroSim sits near the concept it illustrates. After exploring, have learners re-read the surrounding text.
    5. Internet required. MicroSims load from the website. For offline use, build the site locally (see "Customizing Your Own Textbook" below).

    Embedding a MicroSim in Your Own Page¶

    You can add any MicroSim to a Google Site, a WordPress blog, a learning management system (LMS) such as Canvas or Schoology, or a plain HTML file. Paste one line of HTML:

    <iframe src="https://dmccreary.github.io/oer-evaluator/sims/YOUR-MICROSIM-NAME/main.html"
    width="100%" height="450px"
    scrolling="no">
</iframe>

    Replace YOUR-MICROSIM-NAME with the folder name of any MicroSim, for example cc-license-switches. Some LMS platforms block iframes. If yours does, link to the MicroSim page instead.

    MicroSim Specifications¶

    Each MicroSim has its own lesson page with a description, the learning objective, the controls, and lesson-plan ideas. Use these pages for planning and to see the teaching intent behind each simulation.

    Using the Glossary¶

    The glossary is an alphabetical list of the book's 351 key terms, each with a short, precise definition. Learners reach it from the Glossary link in the left navigation or from the search bar at the top of any page. The browser's find command (Ctrl+F or Cmd+F) finds a term on the glossary page.

    Ways to use it in class:

    • Vocabulary preview. Before a chapter, have learners look up its key terms.
    • Definition matching. Run a warm-up where learners match definitions to terms.
    • Student-written definitions. Learners write their own definitions after reading, then compare them with the glossary.
    • Quick quizzes. Use terms for flash cards or short formative checks.

    Using the FAQ¶

    The FAQ (Frequently Asked Questions) holds 100 questions that learners and reviewers commonly ask about evaluating OER, grouped by topic. Answers are written at the same reading level as the chapters.

    • Discussion starters. Pick two or three questions, have learners discuss, then reveal the answer.
    • Homework support. Point learners to the FAQ for questions outside class.
    • Extension reading. The FAQ covers angles that the chapter text skips.
    • Test review. Learners can use it as a study guide.

    Using the Quizzes¶

    Every chapter has a quiz page with ten multiple-choice questions, 190 in all. Open the Quiz link under a chapter in the left navigation. Learners click a question to reveal the answer and explanation after trying it.

    Quizzes are not graded automatically. They are formative self-checks, not summative tests.

    • Exit tickets. Have learners take the quiz at the end of a session.
    • Pre-reading check. Assign it before the chapter to see what learners already know.
    • Post-reading review. Use it to find concepts that need re-teaching.
    • Pair discussion. Learners discuss each question before revealing the answer.
    • Question bank. Reuse the questions in your own assessments. The license permits it, with attribution and under the same license.

    Bloom's Taxonomy Levels¶

    Each quiz question is tagged with a Bloom's Taxonomy level. Bloom's Taxonomy classifies thinking skills from simple to complex:

    Level Name What It Means Example Verbs
    L1 Remember Recall facts and definitions Define, list, name
    L2 Understand Explain concepts in your own words Explain, describe, compare
    L3 Apply Use concepts to solve problems Calculate, demonstrate, solve
    L4 Analyze Break down and examine relationships Differentiate, organize
    L5 Evaluate Make judgments based on criteria Assess, justify, critique
    L6 Create Produce original work Design, construct, propose

    The quizzes target mostly levels L1 to L4. Rubric scoring practice, agent-output checks, and the A/B test design in the later chapters reach L5 and L6.

    Using the References¶

    Each chapter has an Annotated References page with about ten sources for further reading. Each entry gives the title, a link, and a note on why the source is useful. Wikipedia articles come first for stability and access, followed by authoritative books and papers.

    A Note About Link Rot¶

    Link rot happens when a web link stops working because the page moved or was deleted. Wikipedia links are stable, but some others may age. If a link is broken:

    1. Search for the article title on the source's website.
    2. Look for an archived copy in the Wayback Machine.
    3. Report it as a GitHub Issue (see "Feedback" below).

    A good exercise

    Checking whether a link still works is itself an OER evaluation skill (accuracy and currency, Chapter 5). Ask learners to audit a reference page.

    Using the Learning Graph¶

    What Is a Learning Graph?¶

    A learning graph is a map showing how the concepts in a textbook depend on each other. It is a DAG (Directed Acyclic Graph): a diagram of nodes joined by one-way arrows, with no loops. An arrow from concept A to concept B means "understand A before B."

    This book's graph has 351 concepts in 14 categories. Open the Learning Graph section of the left navigation to explore it and to see the quality reports.

    How Teachers Can Use the Learning Graph¶

    • Prerequisite checking. Before teaching a concept, confirm that learners have covered what it depends on.
    • Remediation. When a learner struggles, trace back through the prerequisites to find the gap.
    • Curriculum mapping. Compare the graph with your syllabus to find coverage gaps.
    • Enrichment. Advanced learners can follow the graph forward to concepts beyond the current chapter.

    Echo the Loon: The Pedagogical Agent¶

    A pedagogical agent is a character who appears in a textbook to guide learners. Research on the persona effect suggests that such characters improve engagement and learners' perception of the material.

    This book's agent is Echo the Loon, who carries a magnifying glass and asks, "What is the evidence?" Echo's catchphrase is "Let's dive in and check the evidence!" See Meet Echo for the full character sheet.

    Echo appears as colored callout boxes (called admonitions) in student chapters. There are seven types:

    Type Purpose Where It Appears
    Welcome Introduces the chapter Chapter opening
    Thinking Highlights a key insight Occasionally
    Tip Shares practical advice As the material warrants
    Warning Alerts to a common mistake As the material warrants
    Encourage Supports learners on harder concepts Where learners may struggle
    Celebration Marks progress Chapter ending
    Neutral General note Rarely

    Echo is used sparingly and never twice in a row. This instructor-facing guide does not use Echo.

    Tips for teachers:

    • Read Echo's tips aloud. They are written in a calm, conversational voice.
    • Use "thinking" boxes as discussion prompts. They mark the most important insights.
    • Point frustrated learners to "encourage" boxes.

    The Mascot Rendering Test page shows all seven styles.

    Feedback¶

    Reporting Issues and Suggestions¶

    This book is an open-source project on GitHub, a website where projects are built collaboratively. You do not need to program to report a problem.

    A GitHub Issue works like a support ticket. Each issue has a number and can be discussed by the author and the community.

    1. Go to the GitHub repository.
    2. Click the Issues tab.
    3. Click the green New issue button.
    4. Give a clear title, such as "Broken link in Chapter 5 references".
    5. In the description, say which page has the problem, what you expected, what you saw, and your browser and device if relevant.
    6. Click Submit new issue.

    A free GitHub account is required. If you prefer not to make one, use the Contact page.

    Welcome feedback includes typos and factual errors, broken links, MicroSim bugs, content suggestions, and accessibility problems.

    A license is a legal document that says what others may do with a work. A Creative Commons (CC) license is a standard, plain-language license used for educational and creative content. This is the same kind of license that the book teaches learners to read.

    Customizing Your Own Textbook¶

    You can make your own customized copy. This section explains how, step by step.

    Key Technical Terms¶

    • Repository (repo). A folder on GitHub that holds all of a project's files.
    • Git. A tool that tracks changes to files and who made them.
    • Clone. A complete copy of a repository on your own computer.
    • Fork. A complete copy of a repository in your own GitHub account.
    • MkDocs. The software that turns the book's Markdown files into a website.
    • Markdown. A simple text format. **bold** makes bold, # Heading makes a heading, and - makes a bullet point.
    • mkdocs.yml. The main settings file for the site. It controls the title, navigation, colors, and enabled features.

    Step 1: Create a GitHub Account¶

    If you do not have one, create a free account at github.com.

    Step 2: Fork or Clone the Repository¶

    Option A: Fork (easier, stays on GitHub)

    1. Go to the repository.
    2. Click Fork in the upper right.
    3. You now have a copy in your own account that you can edit.

    Option B: Clone (more control, works on your computer)

    1. Install Git from git-scm.com.
    2. Open a terminal (Command Prompt on Windows, Terminal on Mac).
    3. Run:
    git clone https://github.com/dmccreary/oer-evaluator.git

    Step 3: Make Changes¶

    All content lives in the docs/ folder as Markdown (.md) files. Edit them with any text editor.

    Change the Title and Description¶

    Edit these lines in mkdocs.yml:

    site_name: "Your Custom Textbook Title"
site_description: "Your description here"
site_author: "Your Name"

    Change the Colors¶

    Find the palette section in mkdocs.yml:

    theme:
  palette:
    primary: indigo    # try blue, red, purple, teal, green, and others
    accent: orange     # the accent color

    Change the Logo¶

    Replace the logo file under docs/img/ with your own PNG image, about 128 by 128 pixels.

    Keep the license notice

    If you share your customized version, keep the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 notice and credit the original author.

    Step 4: Preview Your Changes¶

    1. Install Python (3.8 or newer) from python.org.

    2. Install MkDocs and the Material theme:

      pip install mkdocs mkdocs-material

    3. In the project folder, start the preview server:

      cd oer-evaluator
mkdocs serve

    4. Open http://127.0.0.1:8000/oer-evaluator/ in your browser.

    The preview refreshes each time you save a file.

    Step 5: Publish Your Version¶

    To publish as a free website with GitHub Pages:

    mkdocs gh-deploy

    This publishes to https://YOUR-USERNAME.github.io/oer-evaluator/ in about one to two minutes.

    Customizing Your Analytics¶

    What Is Web Analytics?¶

    Web analytics measures how visitors use a website: which pages they visit, how long they stay, and where they come from. For a textbook, it shows which chapters are read and which MicroSims are used.

    Google Analytics¶

    This book uses Google Analytics, a free Google service. The author's property is already set up. If you make your own copy, create your own so that your learners' visits are not mixed in with the author's.

    1. Go to analytics.google.com and sign in.
    2. Create a property (Google's word for a tracked website).
    3. Google gives you a Measurement ID that looks like G-XXXXXXXXXX.

    4. Put it in mkdocs.yml:

      extra:
  analytics:
    provider: google
    property: G-YOUR-MEASUREMENT-ID

    5. Rebuild and deploy. Data appears within 24 to 48 hours.

    What you can learn: most and least visited chapters, time on page, phone versus computer use, and where visitors are located.

    xAPI Monitoring (Advanced)¶

    xAPI (Experience API) is a standard for recording detailed learning activity, such as "the learner answered question 3 correctly." An LRS (Learning Record Store) is the database that holds those records.

    Student privacy laws apply

    Before collecting data that can identify individual learners, know the rules that apply to you:

    • FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act), a U.S. law that protects student education records.
    • COPPA (Children's Online Privacy Protection Act), a U.S. law with extra limits for children under 13.
    • State laws. Many U.S. states add their own student privacy rules.
    • GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), a European Union law that applies if any learner is in the EU.

    The Google Analytics setup above is anonymous by default: it counts page views, not individual people. That is the safest choice. If you want per-learner tracking, talk to your school or district data privacy officer first.
